# Chapter 4.3 — Climate, Sustainability, and Environmental Public Value

**Climate change forces public value theory to answer a question markets cannot price and electorates cannot fully authorize: how much is owed, today, to people who do not yet exist, cannot vote, and cannot object to the bill being left for them.**

## Why this matters in public value

Every public body now sits inside two overlapping obligations that pull against each other on the same budget line. One is to the people who elected it, fund it, or govern it this year, whose immediate needs are visible and politically consequential. The other is to a future population — some already alive as children, some not yet born — whose interests are real but structurally unable to compete in this year's consultation or spending review. Climate change is the sharpest version of this problem a public manager will meet, because the costs of inaction compound on a timescale that outlasts any single administration, while the costs of action land now, on budgets that are already contested.

The economist Nicholas Stern's influential 2006 review for the UK government captured the asymmetry starkly: the economic and human costs of unmitigated climate change would eventually dwarf the cost of preventing them, yet the prevention spending has to be authorized by decision-makers who will mostly not live to see the worst counterfactual avoided (see the **[Stern Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stern_Review)**, discussed further below). Public value theory exists precisely to give decision-makers language for obligations that are real but not enforced by any market price or any current voter's ballot, which is why this chapter treats climate and environmental questions as core public value questions rather than as a specialist add-on for environment ministries alone.

This chapter separates three distinct but related problems that public leaders routinely blur together. The first is **climate change mitigation** — reducing the greenhouse gas emissions that cause warming — which behaves economically like a pure global public good: one country's reduced emissions benefit the whole planet's atmosphere, whoever contributed them, and no country can be excluded from the benefit or compelled to pay its share (see Chapter 1.4 — Public Goods, Merit Goods, and Value Pluralism for the underlying taxonomy). The second is **climate change adaptation** — adjusting infrastructure, services, and behavior to a changing climate that is already partly locked in — which is far more local: a sea wall protects a specific coastline, and its costs and benefits mostly land within one jurisdiction's boundary. The third is the accounting problem underneath both: how to bring the value of natural systems — forests that store carbon, wetlands that absorb floods, soils that grow food — into decisions that have historically weighed only financial and, more recently, social value. Getting these three problems tangled together is one of the most common reasons a climate strategy stalls: a council that funds only adaptation because mitigation feels like someone else's job is not making a decision so much as avoiding one.

## Core concepts

**[Climate change mitigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_mitigation)**, also called decarbonization, is action to limit the greenhouse gases entering the atmosphere, whether by cutting emissions at source (renewable energy, efficiency, electrification) or by increasing removals (reforestation, carbon capture). Because the atmosphere mixes globally, a tonne of carbon dioxide avoided in one country delivers the same climate benefit wherever it was avoided, which is what makes mitigation the textbook case of a **global public good**: non-rivalrous and functionally non-excludable, since no country can be shut out of a stabilized climate it did not pay to secure. That combination produces the free-rider incentive described in Chapter 1.4 — Public Goods, Merit Goods, and Value Pluralism at a planetary scale, and it is the central reason mitigation has proven so much harder to negotiate collectively than adaptation, whose benefits are largely captured by whoever pays for them.

**[Climate change adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_adaptation)** is the process of adjusting to the effects of climate change, both already occurring and anticipated, across four broad categories: infrastructural (flood defenses, cooling centers), institutional (building codes, early-warning systems), behavioral (changed farming or water-use practices), and nature-based (restored wetlands, urban tree cover). Unlike mitigation, adaptation is substantially excludable and rivalrous — a flood defense protects the town behind it, not the next stretch of coast — which makes it chiefly a local and national public value question, closely related to, but not identical with, the resilience planning in Chapter 3.12 — Crisis, Emergency, and Resilience Management. The two overlap because both concern an organization's capacity to absorb and recover from shocks, but adaptation concerns a changing climate baseline specifically, while resilience management covers the fuller range of shocks, including ones unrelated to climate.

**[Intergenerational equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergenerational_equity)** is the principle of fairness between generations. It matters here because almost every consequential climate decision trades a cost today for a benefit decades away, and how that trade is valued depends heavily on the **social discount rate** used to compare present and future value, developed fully in Chapter 2.2 — Social Cost-Benefit and Cost-Effectiveness Analysis. A higher rate shrinks the present-day weight given to distant benefits, which can make a mitigation program look poor value even when its eventual benefits are enormous; a lower rate gives future generations' welfare closer to equal standing with the present's, at the cost of making more programs look expensive today. The Stern Review's decision to use a markedly low discount rate for climate policy remains one of the most publicly contested applications of discounting in public economics, precisely because the choice of rate is a value judgment about intergenerational fairness dressed up as a technical parameter.

**[Natural capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_capital)** is the stock of the world's natural resources — geology, soils, air, water, and all living organisms — treated, by analogy with financial capital, as an asset yielding a flow of value over time. That flow is made up of **[ecosystem services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_service)**: benefits humans derive from ecosystems, from provisioning services such as timber and fisheries, through regulating services such as flood control and carbon storage, to cultural services such as recreation. **[Natural capital accounting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_capital_accounting)** attempts to measure these stocks and flows in terms that sit alongside financial and social accounts, using frameworks such as the United Nations' System of Environmental-Economic Accounting and the World Bank's Changing Wealth of Nations program, so a wetland's flood-protection value can inform a budget decision the way a financial asset's value already does. This remains an emerging discipline relative to financial accounting's centuries of practice: genuinely useful and rapidly maturing, but not yet fully settled.

The **[social cost of carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_cost_of_carbon)** connects natural capital, discounting, and cost-benefit analysis in one number: an estimate, usually expressed in money per tonne, of the economic damage caused by emitting one additional tonne of carbon dioxide, used the way Chapter 2.2 — Social Cost-Benefit and Cost-Effectiveness Analysis uses shadow prices for other non-market goods. It is extraordinarily sensitive to the discount rate chosen, since most of a tonne's damage lands decades in the future.

Three institutions anchor the global architecture around these concepts. The **[Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_Panel_on_Climate_Change)** (IPCC), a United Nations body, assesses and synthesizes the published science of climate change for policymakers without conducting its own primary research. The **[United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Framework_Convention_on_Climate_Change)** (UNFCCC) is the treaty process through which countries negotiate collective climate action, and the **[Paris Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Agreement)**, adopted under it in 2015, is the current legal framework committing parties to hold warming well below two degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, pursued through nationally determined contributions each country sets and periodically strengthens. Alongside mitigation and adaptation, negotiators increasingly recognize a third category, **[loss and damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_and_damage_(climate_change))**: residual harm that occurs after mitigation has failed to prevent a change and adaptation has failed, or been unable, to absorb it, raising its own equity questions about who bears costs that neither prevention nor adjustment avoided.

## Best practices

1. **Separate the mitigation question from the adaptation question in every strategy document.** Mitigation is a contribution to a global public good whose local benefit is diffuse (see Chapter 1.4 — Public Goods, Merit Goods, and Value Pluralism); adaptation protects a specific, local asset or population. Funding and governance differ sharply between the two, and merging them into one "climate strategy" line routinely leaves one under-resourced.
2. **State the discount rate used in any climate appraisal, and who chose it.** Publish the rate, its source, and a sensitivity analysis showing how the recommendation changes at a lower and a higher rate (see Chapter 2.2 — Social Cost-Benefit and Cost-Effectiveness Analysis).
3. **Treat adaptation as a resilience investment with its own governance, not a subset of emergency response.** Adaptation shares tools with the resilience management in Chapter 3.12 — Crisis, Emergency, and Resilience Management, but needs a standing, multi-year program rather than a crisis-triggered one, since the risks build over decades.
4. **Start a natural capital register for your major land and water assets, even an incomplete one.** A first-pass inventory of the forests, wetlands, and green spaces an organization owns, with a qualitative note on each asset's ecosystem services, is more useful than waiting for a perfect monetized valuation.
5. **Do not let a single social cost of carbon figure stand in for the whole climate case.** The figure is legitimate and useful, but its sensitivity to the discount rate means a decision that only clears a cost-benefit test because of a favorable rate choice deserves extra scrutiny, not automatic approval.
6. **Report climate risk exposure in the same cycle as financial risk, not as an annual annex nobody reads.** Give physical climate risk — flood, heat, drought, storm exposure — the same standing review cadence as financial and cyber risk, reaching the same governance body with the same seriousness.
7. **Name the intergenerational trade-off explicitly in business cases with long time horizons.** Where a decision imposes cost on the current generation for benefit to future ones, or vice versa, state that trade-off in plain language rather than letting it hide inside an uninterrogated discount rate.
8. **Build adaptation plans around the most vulnerable populations first, not the most valuable assets first.** A purely asset-value-weighted plan protects wealthier areas before poorer ones, because asset values are higher there; weight vulnerability and exposure explicitly when sequencing investment.
9. **Coordinate mitigation commitments vertically, from local pledges to nationally determined contributions.** A local net-zero target is only credible if it reconciles with the national government's Paris Agreement commitments; publish how local targets roll up, rather than setting them in isolation.
10. **Distinguish avoided loss and damage from adaptation success in your own reporting.** Loss and damage funding compensates harm that occurred despite mitigation and adaptation; conflating it with an adaptation budget line understates how much protection actually failed.
11. **Pilot natural capital accounting on one asset class before an organization-wide roll-out.** Choose a bounded case — a single catchment or green-space network — to learn the method's practical limits before committing to a full natural capital balance sheet.
12. **Revisit climate assumptions on a fixed cycle, not only after a crisis.** Emissions trajectories and IPCC assessment findings update periodically; schedule a review of the assumptions behind major plans rather than waiting for a flood or heatwave to force the question.

## Questions to discuss with your team

**What discount rate underlies our long-horizon climate decisions, and could we defend it to the people it disadvantages?** Almost every substantial mitigation or adaptation decision embeds a discount rate that determines how much weight future generations' welfare receives relative to the present generation's. Ask where your organization's rate came from — borrowed guidance, deliberately set for climate work, or never actually decided at all. Discuss what happens to your headline climate business cases at a meaningfully lower rate, closer to the near-zero pure time preference the Stern Review used, or a higher, conventional infrastructure rate. If the ranking flips under a plausible alternative, that instability is something your authorizing environment needs to see (see Chapter 2.2 — Social Cost-Benefit and Cost-Effectiveness Analysis).

**Are we funding mitigation as though it were someone else's job, because its benefits are diffuse and global?** Mitigation's status as a global public good means any single organization's contribution delivers only a tiny, diffuse share of benefit back to itself, creating a real temptation to under-invest and let others carry the cost. Walk through your commitments and ask honestly whether they match your fair contribution to a shared global effort, or merely what looks defensible against comparable peers. Discuss what a credible, rather than merely comparable, level of ambition looks like for an organization your size (see Chapter 1.4 — Public Goods, Merit Goods, and Value Pluralism).

**Whose vulnerability, not whose assets, is driving our adaptation priorities?** An adaptation plan built around protecting the highest-value property will systematically favor wealthier areas, because that is where asset values concentrate, leaving poorer, more exposed populations under-protected. Review your current or draft sequencing and ask what weight, if any, it gives to social vulnerability rather than asset value alone. Discuss who would be first and last to receive protection under the current plan, and whether that order is defensible in public.

**Do we know what natural capital we are responsible for, and what it is doing for us?** Most organizations that own or influence land, water, or green infrastructure have never inventoried the ecosystem services those assets provide, from flood attenuation to carbon storage. Discuss what a first-pass natural capital register would need to cover, who would own it, and what decision it would inform once built — a register nobody consults is not worth building. Consider starting with one bounded asset class rather than a comprehensive valuation immediately.

**How do we handle the difference between adaptation that worked and loss and damage that means it did not?** Adaptation and loss and damage can look similar in a budget line, but they mean very different things about how well-prepared an organization actually was. Discuss whether your reporting distinguishes "we adapted successfully" from "harm occurred that our adaptation did not prevent, and we are now recovering from it", and what it would take to make that distinction visible to your governing body rather than blurred into one total.

**When did we last update the climate assumptions behind our major long-term plans, and against what evidence?** Emissions trajectories, hazard projections, and the scientific assessments behind them change over time, and a plan built on older assumptions may already be materially out of date. Identify the major long-horizon plans — infrastructure, land use, flood defense, service continuity — that rest on a climate assumption, and check when each was last tested against current evidence, including the latest Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change assessment cycle. Agree who owns that review cycle, so plans do not go stale by default.

## In practice: a public value example

Consider a fictional river-delta region, the Meridian Delta Authority, a regional public body responsible for flood defense and land-use planning across a low-lying agricultural and urban area crossed by three rivers. For years, its capital plan had ranked projects on a single benefit-cost ratio, computed at the region's standard infrastructure discount rate, inherited unchanged from a decade-old national Treasury template designed for road-building appraisals.

Under that ranking, a proposed hard flood wall around the region's largest town consistently outscored a competing proposal to restore 600 hectares of upstream wetland that would slow and store floodwater naturally while also sequestering carbon and supporting fisheries. The wall's benefits arrived almost immediately after construction and were valued using well-established property and infrastructure appraisal methods. The wetland's benefits built up over a decade as vegetation matured, and a meaningful share of them — carbon storage, biodiversity, long-run floodplain resilience — had never been priced at all, because the authority had no natural capital accounting practice and simply left those benefits out of the sum rather than assign them a value of zero, which quietly understated the wetland option's case without anyone deciding to do so.

A newly appointed head of strategy asked what the standard appraisal had never separately answered: what discount rate was actually appropriate for a decision whose benefits would extend for at least fifty years, and what would the wetland option's case look like if its ecosystem services were estimated rather than silently treated as worth nothing. Working with an environmental economist, the team produced a first-pass natural capital estimate for the wetland, drawing on published transfer values for comparable floodplain restoration schemes, explicitly labeled as an estimate with a wide plausible range. They also re-ran the appraisal at a lower discount rate reflecting the region's own multi-decade flood-risk horizon, and reported both rankings side by side rather than picking one and hiding the other.

Under the original assumptions, the hard wall still won comfortably. Under the revised assumptions, the two options were close enough that the choice could no longer be presented as a straightforward efficiency verdict, and became a genuine judgment call for the authority's governing board, who also weighed that the wetland scheme built long-run resilience the wall alone did not, while the wall protected the town center faster but did nothing for the agricultural land further upstream. The board chose a hybrid: a smaller hard-defense scheme for the town center, funded on its own merits, alongside a phased wetland restoration funded partly through a national ecosystem-restoration grant and partly by counting its carbon storage toward the authority's own mitigation target. The head of strategy's account of the episode was blunt: the original appraisal was not dishonest, but it had been quietly making value judgments — about discounting and about what counts as a benefit at all — that nobody in the room had actually chosen to make.

## Four sector lenses

### Local government

Local and regional authorities carry most of the practical burden of adaptation, because flood defense, urban heat management, water supply resilience, and local land-use planning are typically local or regional competencies, closely bound to the resilience work in Chapter 3.12 — Crisis, Emergency, and Resilience Management. Their distinctive challenge is capital scale against risk scale: a single local authority often faces flood or heat risk large enough to require national or multilateral co-funding, and its discount rate and appraisal method are frequently borrowed from national guidance not designed with a multi-decade local adaptation horizon in mind.

### National government

National governments set the mitigation commitments that make up a country's contribution to the Paris Agreement, hold the authoritative discount-rate guidance that regional and local appraisals inherit (see Chapter 2.2 — Social Cost-Benefit and Cost-Effectiveness Analysis), and increasingly experiment with natural capital accounting at the national accounts level, following frameworks like the United Nations' System of Environmental-Economic Accounting. Their central tension is between international climate commitments negotiated at speed in multilateral forums and the domestic political economy of who bears the near-term cost of meeting them.

### Social sector and nonprofit

Nonprofits and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) frequently deliver adaptation on the ground in communities least able to self-fund it — community flood preparedness, heat-resilient shelter, climate-smart agriculture extension — and act as advocates pressing loss and damage and climate justice claims on behalf of populations with little formal voice in national or multilateral negotiations. Their legitimacy in this space rests on proximity to affected communities and mission credibility rather than formal authority, which means their most valuable contribution is often naming a vulnerability or an injustice that a government appraisal, built around aggregate cost-benefit ratios, would not otherwise surface.

### Multilateral and international

Mitigation is the paradigm case of a genuinely global public good discussed in Chapter 1.4 — Public Goods, Merit Goods, and Value Pluralism, which is why the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change process, and the Paris Agreement exist: to coordinate a collective-action problem that no single state can solve unilaterally and that leaves every state with an incentive to let others act first. Multilateral development institutions, including the World Bank, are also the main drivers of standardizing natural capital accounting across countries at very different income levels and data capacities, and of structuring the emerging loss and damage finance architecture between higher- and lower-emitting nations.

## Common failure modes

- **The borrowed discount rate.** Climate and infrastructure appraisals routinely inherit a discount rate designed for short-horizon road or building projects, which systematically undervalues the long-run benefits of mitigation and adaptation without anyone deliberately deciding that future generations should count for less.
- **Mitigation as someone else's responsibility.** Because mitigation's benefits are diffuse and global, individual organizations under-invest relative to their fair share, reasoning implicitly that a small, local contribution to a planetary problem is not worth the local cost — a rational-looking calculation for any one actor that is collectively ruinous if every actor makes it.
- **Adaptation sequenced by asset value, not by vulnerability.** Protecting the most expensive property first, rather than the most exposed population first, quietly reproduces existing inequality under the banner of efficient resource allocation.
- **Silent zero-valuation of natural capital.** Leaving ecosystem services out of an appraisal because they have not been monetized is not neutral; it is equivalent to assigning them a value of zero, which structurally disadvantages nature-based options against conventional infrastructure every time, without that choice ever being stated or defended.
- **Conflating loss and damage with adaptation spending.** Reporting compensation for harm that adaptation failed to prevent as though it were evidence of adaptation working obscures how much protection actually failed and delays the reckoning about what more prevention would have cost.
- **Stranded plans built on stale assumptions.** Multi-decade infrastructure and land-use plans built on emissions or sea-level assumptions that are years out of date quietly become liabilities, because nobody owns the job of checking whether the underlying climate science has moved since the plan was adopted.

## Maturity model

| Sub-dimension | Initiate | Develop | Standardize | Manage | Orchestrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mitigation and adaptation strategy | No distinction made; climate treated as one undifferentiated line item | Mitigation and adaptation are named separately but not separately resourced | Both have distinct funding, targets, and governance, reconciled with national commitments | Progress against both is measured and reported on a fixed cycle, with corrective action when off track | Targets and delivery are coordinated with peer bodies, national government, and international frameworks |
| Discount rate and intergenerational equity | Rate borrowed ad hoc from unrelated guidance, never questioned | Some teams flag the rate as contestable but use it unchanged | A documented rate, with rationale, is mandated for climate appraisals specifically | Sensitivity analysis at alternative rates is standard practice and reported to decision-makers | Rate-setting is reviewed publicly and reconciled with peer jurisdictions' intergenerational-equity guidance |
| Natural capital accounting | Ecosystem services are not identified or valued at all | A qualitative register exists for some major assets, incomplete and unmonetized | A documented natural capital register, with estimated values, covers all major land and water assets | Natural capital values are used routinely in appraisal alongside financial and social value | Natural capital accounts are integrated with national or international accounting standards and shared across boundaries |
| Vulnerability-led adaptation sequencing | Adaptation investment follows asset value with no vulnerability weighting | Vulnerability is discussed but rarely changes the sequencing in practice | A documented method weights vulnerability and exposure alongside asset value | Sequencing outcomes are monitored for equity effects and adjusted when they diverge from intent | Sequencing is co-designed with the most exposed communities, not only assessed on their behalf |

## Checklist

- [ ] Mitigation and adaptation are budgeted, governed, and reported as distinct programs, not one undifferentiated climate line.
- [ ] The discount rate used in any climate appraisal is stated, sourced, and sensitivity-tested against at least one alternative rate.
- [ ] Intergenerational trade-offs in long-horizon decisions are named explicitly for the authorizing body, not buried in a technical annex.
- [ ] A natural capital register exists, even in first-pass form, for major land and water assets the organization owns or influences.
- [ ] Ecosystem services left out of a monetized appraisal are flagged as excluded, not silently valued at zero.
- [ ] Adaptation investment is sequenced with explicit weight given to social vulnerability, not asset value alone.
- [ ] Loss and damage spending is reported separately from, and not counted as, adaptation success.
- [ ] Local mitigation commitments are reconciled with national nationally determined contributions under the Paris Agreement.
- [ ] Climate assumptions behind major long-term plans are reviewed on a fixed cycle against current scientific assessments.
- [ ] Physical climate risk to assets and services is reported through the same governance channel as financial and operational risk.
