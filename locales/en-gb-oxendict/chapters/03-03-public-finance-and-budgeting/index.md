# Chapter 3.3 — Public Finance and Budgeting

**A budget is not a forecast of what an organization intends to spend but the point at which competing public priorities are ranked, made legally enforceable, and opened to challenge, so how a budget is made determines its legitimacy as much as its arithmetic determines its accuracy.**

## Why this matters in public value

Every finance director who has taken a spending round to a cabinet, a council, or a board has watched the same pattern: departments arrive with more claims than the envelope can hold, each defensible on its own terms, and someone has to decide which lose. That decision is not a technical residual left over after policy is set — it is where policy becomes real. A programme that exists only in a strategy document has made no claim on anyone; a programme with a line in the approved budget has, and that line can be tracked, audited, and defended or attacked.

This is also where the strategic triangle stops being an abstraction. A spending proposal can have strong legitimacy and support, a compelling value proposition, and real operational capacity, and still lose in the budget round to a proposal with less of all three but better placement in the political calendar (see Chapter 1.2 — The Strategic Triangle: Legitimacy, Value, and Capacity). Public finance and budgeting is the arena where that mismatch is either corrected, through a disciplined process that tests claims on their merits, or entrenched, through one that rewards whoever shouted loudest or arrived first.

The stakes have sharpened for almost every type of public and social-sector organization. Slower growth, higher debt-service costs, ageing populations, and climate-adaptation costs have tightened the envelope in most jurisdictions just as citizens expect more from it, so the quality of the budget process, not only the quantity of money in it, increasingly determines whether an organization can still create public value within its means. Getting this right is not bookkeeping bolted onto the real work of public management; taxing and spending are themselves the two most powerful instruments a public body has for creating public value, and the budget is the document that decides how they are used.

## Core concepts

**A budget** is a plan, usually covering a fiscal year, that sets out expected revenue and authorized expenditure for a government, agency, or organization; the [government budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_budget) article on Wikipedia frames it as a projection that doubles as a policy instrument, a dual character running through this chapter. **Public finance** is the broader field studying how governments raise revenue, allocate it, and manage debt, and how those choices affect stability and distribution across society (see [public finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_finance)); this chapter focuses on the narrower operational machinery, the budget process itself.

**The budget cycle** has four recurring phases, and most failures in public finance trace back to a weakness in one specific phase rather than the system as a whole. **Formulation** is the executive-branch stage: a finance ministry or treasury sets a fiscal ceiling, spending bodies bid against it, and the executive negotiates a proposal internally before it becomes public. **Approval** is the legislative stage: the proposal is scrutinized, amended, and enacted, typically through an [appropriation bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appropriation_bill), without which, in most systems, money cannot lawfully leave the treasury; where a legislature misses this deadline, many jurisdictions use a continuing resolution or equivalent stopgap to keep spending authorized at prior-year levels. **Execution** is the stage in which the enacted budget is actually spent: funds are released, commitments tracked against authorized limits, and in-year adjustments made as circumstances change. **Audit** closes the cycle, examining whether money was spent as authorized and, increasingly, whether it achieved what was promised; this chapter treats audit only as the closing phase, since the accountability architecture around it is the home territory of Chapter 3.4 — Accountability, Transparency, and Legitimacy (see Chapter 3.4 — Accountability, Transparency, and Legitimacy).

**Fiscal rules** are numerical constraints on budgetary aggregates, set in law, constitution, or binding policy, intended to prevent a persistent deficit bias — the well-documented tendency of governments to spend more than they raise, especially in election years. The International Monetary Fund's Fiscal Affairs Department classifies fiscal rules into four broad types: budget-balance rules requiring a [balanced budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balanced_budget) or limiting the deficit; debt rules capping [government debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_debt), often expressed as a [debt-to-GDP ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt-to-GDP_ratio) (gross domestic product, GDP, being the standard measure of a country's total economic output); expenditure rules capping spending growth; and revenue rules governing windfall or shortfall revenue. Some jurisdictions embed a balanced-budget requirement directly in constitutional law — a 'balanced budget amendment,' in the term used where this has been debated most publicly — while others rely on ordinary statute or political commitment.

**The trade-off inside every fiscal rule** is between credibility and flexibility. A rigid rule is easier to enforce and harder to evade, which is why it reassures lenders and taxpayers that debt will not spiral; but a rule that forces cuts or tax rises exactly when the economy is weak makes fiscal policy **procyclical** — reinforcing the business cycle — rather than countercyclical, the pattern generally preferred for [macroeconomic stabilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procyclical_and_countercyclical_variables). The usual remedy is a pre-defined **escape clause**: stated conditions, such as a recession or declared emergency, under which the rule is formally suspended rather than quietly ignored, so flexibility survives without destroying credibility for the next shock.

**A medium-term expenditure framework (MTEF)** links the single-year budget to a rolling multi-year fiscal plan, typically three to five years, so a decision this year is made with visibility of its consequences in the years that follow. In its standard form, an MTEF combines a top-down resource envelope — what the state can plausibly spend given its fiscal rule and revenue forecast — with a bottom-up costing of current commitments, reconciling the two before allocating headroom to new priorities. South Africa introduced one of the first MTEFs developed independently of donor pressure, in its 1998 budget, and the World Bank subsequently built the approach into its standard public-finance guidance for governments generally; without one, a budget can approve a programme that is unaffordable in year three of a five-year commitment, because nothing in the process was designed to notice.

**Incremental budgeting** takes the prior year's allocation as the starting point and negotiates only the size of the change, rather than re-justifying the base. [Incrementalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incrementalism) is well documented in public budgeting because it is administratively cheap and politically manageable — every established programme has defenders, and negotiating only the margin avoids reopening every fight every year — but it also lets an obsolete programme, once embedded in the baseline, survive indefinitely because no part of the process ever asks whether it should exist at all.

**Zero-based budgeting** answers that weakness by requiring every programme, in principle, to justify its full funding from a baseline of zero each cycle rather than only its increment. Developed as a management technique in the 1970s (see [zero-based budgeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-based_budgeting)), it is powerful but expensive to run properly across an entire budget every year, so most organizations that use it seriously apply it selectively, to one sector on a rotating basis, rather than to everything at once.

**Programme and performance budgeting** classifies spending by what it is meant to achieve — a programme or output — rather than only by the traditional line-item chart of accounts (salaries, supplies, capital). [Performance-based budgeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance-based_budgeting) links funding levels, at least notionally, to expected or achieved results, shifting the conversation from 'how much did this cost' to 'what did this buy,' though the link between performance data and the actual allocation decision is often looser than the label implies, and can create the perverse-incentive problems Chapter 3.11 — Quality, Safety, and Performance Management examines in depth (see Chapter 3.11 — Quality, Safety, and Performance Management).

**Tax and spending as instruments, not just accounting entries**, is the frame this chapter insists on throughout. [Fiscal policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_policy) treats both sides of the budget as active levers: a tax credit for childcare and a direct spending programme for childcare can pursue the same public value goal through different instruments, and a competent budget process compares them on that basis rather than treating tax as a fixed backdrop to a spending decision. **Deficit spending**, where expenditure exceeds revenue in a period, is one such lever rather than simply a failure of discipline; the genuine debate, reflected in the Wikipedia article on [deficit spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deficit_spending), is over when and how much it is appropriate, not whether it is always wrong.

## Best practices

1. **Set the medium-term ceiling before the allocation fight starts.** Agree the top-down resource envelope against the fiscal rule and revenue forecast first, so departments negotiate shares of a known total rather than treating the total itself as negotiable.
2. **Write a genuine escape clause into every fiscal rule before it is needed.** A rule suspended informally in a crisis, with no pre-agreed trigger, damages credibility more than a rule with a clear, narrow, pre-stated exception ever would.
3. **Cost new policy commitments across the full medium-term period, not just the first year.** A proposal that looks affordable in year one and unaffordable in year three should be visible as such before approval, not discovered during the next cycle.
4. **Require an economic case for major new spending, built to the same discipline as a capital business case.** Route significant new programmes through the appraisal method Chapter 2.5 — Business Cases and Value for Money sets out, so a spending bid and an investment bid face comparable scrutiny (see Chapter 2.5 — Business Cases and Value for Money).
5. **Classify spending by programme and output alongside the traditional line-item chart of accounts.** Keep the accounting classification for control and audit, but add a programme view so decision-makers can see what an allocation is meant to achieve, not only what it will be spent on.
6. **Run a genuine zero-based review of at least one major spending area each cycle, on a rotating basis.** Reviewing everything from zero every year is rarely affordable; reviewing nothing from zero, ever, lets obsolete programmes survive on inertia alone.
7. **Give budget holders real in-year flexibility within clear limits.** Reasonable virement — the authority to move funds between budget lines without a fresh appropriation — lets managers respond to changing conditions without breaching legislative control of the totals.
8. **Track execution against the approved budget monthly, not only at year-end.** A large gap between what was approved and what was actually spent, discovered only in the audit, is a governance failure that monthly in-year reporting would have caught in time to correct.
9. **Align tax-expenditure estimates with direct-spending totals in the same document.** A tax relief that reduces revenue by the same amount a spending programme would cost is a spending decision in substance; publishing it separately hides its true size from scrutiny.
10. **Reconcile outturn against the original approved budget and publish the variance.** A published, explained gap between what was approved and what was spent is the single most useful discipline for improving next year's formulation.

## Questions to discuss with your team

**Does our process fix the total envelope before departments compete for shares of it, or does the sum of departmental bids effectively become the envelope?** Many budget processes claim to work top-down but function bottom-up, because the finance ministry lacks the political weight to hold a ceiling against determined bids. If the envelope is porous, the fiscal rule behind it is porous too, however firm it looks on paper. Use this question to trace what actually happened to the ceiling in the last two cycles: was it held, or did it move to accommodate the bids that arrived. Discuss what would need to change, politically as much as technically, for it to hold next time.

**What would trigger our fiscal rule's escape clause, and who has the authority to invoke it?** A rule without a stated trigger tends to be suspended informally under pressure, which costs it credibility for the next crisis even if the suspension itself was reasonable. Use this question to find the actual wording, if any exists, and test it against a plausible near-term shock — a recession, a natural disaster, a sudden revenue shortfall. Discuss whether the trigger is specific enough to be invoked quickly and narrow enough not to become a permanent loophole, and who currently has the authority to pull it.

**How much of this year's budget is genuinely new reasoning, and how much is last year's baseline carried forward?** Incremental budgeting is not automatically a failure — it is administratively efficient and it is how most organizations actually operate most of the time — but a budget that is almost entirely baseline plus a small increment has not tested most of its spending against current priorities in years. Use this question to estimate, even roughly, what share of the current budget was substantively reopened this cycle versus rolled over. Discuss which programmes have not had a genuine reprioritization test in the longest time, and whether that is a deliberate choice or an accident of process design.

**Where does a business case's economic case actually land in our budget decision, and does the budget process ever overrule it?** A rigorous economic case, built the way Chapter 2.5 describes, can still lose to a proposal with weaker analysis but stronger political backing, and that is sometimes the right outcome and sometimes a sign the budget process is not really using the analysis it commissions. Use this question to find a recent case where the economic ranking and the funding decision diverged, and establish why. Discuss whether that divergence was made explicit and defensible at the time, or whether it was simply absorbed without comment.

**If a legislator, councillor, or citizen asked how a specific programme's spending compares with what it delivered, could we answer within days, using data from this budget year, not last year's audit?** Execution data that only becomes visible at year-end, through the audit cycle Chapter 3.4 examines, arrives too late to change anything about the year it describes. Use this question to test how current the organization's in-year spending and performance data actually are, in practice, not in policy. Discuss what would need to be true of the finance and performance-reporting systems for that answer to be available within days rather than months.

**Do we understand our medium-term fiscal trajectory well enough that this year's decision does not quietly foreclose next year's options?** A commitment that is affordable this year can still consume so much of next year's headroom that it eliminates choices before anyone has explicitly decided to eliminate them. Use this question to look at the multi-year costing, if one exists, for the two or three largest commitments made in the current budget. Discuss whether anyone in the room today would have made the same decision knowing its full medium-term cost at the time, and if the multi-year costing does not exist, discuss what it would take to build it.

## In practice: a public value example

The finance directorate of a mid-sized coastal city — fictional, illustrative of a recurring pattern rather than a report on any real municipality — faced a structural problem heading into its annual budget cycle: property-tax revenue, the city's largest source, had grown more slowly than costs for three consecutive years, while a state-level balanced-budget rule barred the city from carrying a deficit into the next fiscal year. The city had adopted a three-year medium-term expenditure framework two cycles earlier, which meant the shortfall was visible well before it became a crisis, rather than discovered during formulation itself.

The executive's formulation process opened with a stark choice: hold the balanced-budget rule by cutting discretionary spending across the board, or invoke the rule's narrow escape clause — available only for a state-declared fiscal emergency, never previously used by the city — and run a bounded, one-year deficit while restructuring costs. An across-the-board cut was administratively simple, but the finance director's team modelled its effect on two programmes: a subsidized transit route serving a low-income district with few car owners, and a stormwater-drainage upgrade already two years behind schedule after a previous round of exactly this kind of uniform cut.

Rather than repeat the uniform-cut approach, the city ran a zero-based review of its transport and infrastructure portfolio — its first genuine zero-based exercise — while a separate team built the economic case for the stormwater upgrade to the same discipline its capital business cases already required. The review found the transit subsidy, assessed from zero rather than from its existing baseline, remained justified on equity grounds even under tight finances, while two lower-ridership routes added incrementally over previous years no longer met any documented need and had simply never been reopened for scrutiny. Retiring them freed enough of the budget to close the remaining gap without invoking the escape clause, and the stormwater upgrade proceeded on schedule.

The council approved a budget that held the balanced-budget rule, funded the equity-justified transit route in full, retired the two routes a zero-based test could not justify, and kept the infrastructure project on schedule. The finance director's report to council credited the outcome to medium-term visibility of the revenue trend well before it became a crisis, and to a zero-based process rigorous enough to distinguish a programme worth protecting from one that had simply never been asked to justify itself again.

## Four sector lenses

### Local government

Local authorities typically cannot borrow to cover an operating deficit the way a national government can, and many operate under an explicit balanced-budget requirement imposed by a higher tier of government, which makes the fiscal-rule trade-off between credibility and flexibility unusually direct and local. Revenue is often concentrated in one or two sources — commonly a property tax or a shared national transfer — so a downturn in either has an immediate effect a more diversified national tax base can sometimes absorb. Execution discipline matters intensely because reserves are thin: a local authority that finds a large variance against its approved budget late in the year has far less room to manage it than a national treasury with deeper reserves and borrowing capacity.

### National government

National government is usually where the full apparatus of this chapter is most developed — a dedicated finance ministry or treasury, a statutory or constitutional fiscal rule, a formal medium-term expenditure framework, and independent fiscal institutions assessing compliance. The risk here is less that the machinery is absent and more that it becomes an end in itself: a sophisticated framework can create the appearance of discipline while off-budget vehicles, contingent liabilities, or optimistic revenue forecasts quietly undermine the numbers it reports. National government is also where fiscal policy's role as a macroeconomic instrument, not only an accounting exercise, is most visible, since national tax and spending decisions move the wider economy in a way a single local authority's budget rarely does alone.

### Social sector and nonprofit

Nonprofits and non-governmental organizations do not pass an appropriation bill through a legislature, but they face a structurally similar problem: money is often restricted by individual grants or donor agreements, arrives on each funder's own cycle rather than the organization's fiscal year, and can leave an organization technically solvent while unable to spend on the one activity it most needs to fund. The nonprofit analogue of a medium-term expenditure framework is a multi-year funding pipeline view that tracks committed, probable, and at-risk revenue against multi-year commitments, since a single-year budget can look balanced while masking a funding cliff the year after next — a distinction Chapter 3.8 — Social Sector and Nonprofit Management explores from the funding-model side (see Chapter 3.8 — Social Sector and Nonprofit Management). Unrestricted reserves function, in effect, as this sector's escape clause: an organization holding meaningful reserves can absorb a funding shock without an immediate programme cut, while one without them has no equivalent buffer.

### Multilateral and international

Multilateral bodies typically fund core operations through periodic replenishment rounds, in which member states negotiate several years of contributions at once — a medium-term expenditure framework negotiated among sovereign funders rather than set internally by a finance ministry. Approval sits with a governing board representing member states rather than a domestic legislature, which changes the legitimacy dynamics Chapter 1.2 describes without removing the underlying tension between a fixed envelope and competing claims on it. Because contributions are voluntary rather than compulsory taxation, a multilateral body's effective fiscal rule is less a legal ceiling than a credibility constraint: an organization seen to have used past funding poorly finds its next replenishment round harder, a form of ex post discipline national budgeting achieves through statute instead.

## Common failure modes

**Baseline creep.** Incremental negotiation, repeated year after year, lets an obsolete or underperforming programme survive indefinitely because no stage of the process ever asks whether it should exist, only how much its allocation should change. The cost is a budget that reflects the accumulated history of past political settlements more than current priorities, discovered only when a fiscal shock finally forces the question no ordinary cycle asked.

**Rule evasion through off-budget vehicles.** A government facing a binding fiscal rule sometimes moves spending or borrowing into a separate entity or contingent liability the rule does not formally cover, satisfying its letter while defeating its purpose. The cost surfaces later, often at a moment of stress, when the true scale of the state's obligations proves far larger than the headline figures the rule was designed to police ever showed.

**Procyclical austerity under a rigid rule.** A balanced-budget or deficit rule with no functioning escape clause forces spending cuts or tax rises exactly when a recession has already weakened the economy, deepening the downturn the rule was never designed to cause. The cost is a longer, deeper contraction than a rule with genuine flexibility would have produced, plus the credibility the rule itself loses when it is suspended anyway, informally and late.

**A medium-term expenditure framework that binds nothing.** Some organizations produce a multi-year fiscal forecast because good practice requires one, then negotiate each year's actual budget as though it did not exist, treating the framework as a compliance document rather than a genuine constraint. The cost is that the framework's main value — forcing this year's choice to reckon with next year's consequence — never materializes, even though a document that looks like an MTEF exists.

**Zero-based budgeting used as cover for an across-the-board cut.** A genuine zero-based review distinguishes what is worth funding from what is not; a rushed one, under time pressure, defaults to a uniform percentage cut applied regardless of merit, administratively easier but abandoning the entire rationale for doing zero-based work. The cost falls on programmes that were both effective and easy to cut politically, while weaker programmes with entrenched constituencies survive largely untouched, and the organization learns nothing about which spending genuinely deserved protection.

## Maturity model

| Sub-dimension | Initiate | Develop | Standardize | Manage | Orchestrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Medium-term expenditure framework shapes the annual budget | No multi-year fiscal plan exists; each budget is built from scratch | A multi-year forecast exists but does not bind the annual allocation | A rolling multi-year ceiling is set before sector negotiations and generally holds | New commitments are formally tested against the full medium-term period before approval | Medium-term ceilings are coordinated with other levels of government and delivery partners |
| Fiscal rule is credible and specific | No fiscal rule, or an informal one with no defined trigger for exceptions | A rule exists but its escape-clause conditions are vague or untested | A statutory or constitutional rule with a stated, narrow escape clause is in force | Compliance is tracked and reported against the rule in-year, not only at year-end | An independent fiscal institution monitors and publicly assesses compliance |
| Budgeting method matches the decision it supports | Purely incremental; no programme or output view of spending exists | A programme classification exists alongside the line-item chart of accounts but is rarely used in decisions | Programme and performance data are a standard input to allocation decisions | At least one major spending area undergoes genuine zero-based review each cycle | Budgeting method is adapted deliberately by decision type, with evidence on which method works where |
| Execution is tracked and reconciled against the approved budget | Variance between approved and actual spend is discovered only at year-end audit | In-year monitoring exists but is inconsistent across spending units | Monthly execution reporting against the approved budget is standard practice | Significant variances trigger a defined corrective process during the year, not after it | Execution data is published and used by external stakeholders in near real time |
| Tax and spending decisions are considered together | Tax measures and spending measures are prepared by separate processes that rarely meet | Tax expenditures are estimated but reported separately from direct spending | Tax expenditures and direct spending are presented together in the same budget document | Tax and spending options are compared on a common appraisal basis for a given policy goal | Tax and spending instruments are actively co-designed to pursue the same public value objective |

## Checklist

- [ ] Publish the budget calendar for the full cycle before formulation begins.
- [ ] Set the medium-term resource envelope before departments negotiate their shares of it.
- [ ] State the fiscal rule, and its escape-clause trigger, in law or binding policy, not informal custom.
- [ ] Cost every major new commitment across the full medium-term period, not only its first year.
- [ ] Require an economic case for significant new spending, consistent with Chapter 2.5's method.
- [ ] Classify spending by programme and output alongside the traditional line-item chart of accounts.
- [ ] Run a genuine zero-based review of at least one major spending area this cycle.
- [ ] Track execution against the approved budget monthly, and investigate significant variances during the year.
- [ ] Reconcile year-end outturn against the original approved budget and publish the variance.
- [ ] Present tax-expenditure estimates alongside direct-spending totals in the same document.
- [ ] Confirm who has authority to invoke the fiscal rule's escape clause, and under what stated conditions.
