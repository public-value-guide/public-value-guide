# Chapter 4.2 — Trust in Government and Civic Engagement

**Institutions that citizens judge competent, well-meaning, and honest secure compliance and cooperation at a fraction of the cost of institutions that must instead be policed, because trust is not a mood to be managed with better messaging but an asset built or spent through what an organization actually does.**

## Why this matters in public value

A tax authority that citizens trust collects more revenue with fewer audits. A public health service that citizens trust achieves higher vaccination and screening rates with less coercion. A local council that citizens trust can ask residents to sort waste, conserve water, or accept a new development with far less friction than one that must inspect, fine, and litigate its way to the same result. Trust is not a soft outcome sitting alongside the hard business of government; it is a production input that lowers the cost of nearly everything a public organization tries to do, from tax collection to regulation to emergency response.

That input has been depleting in many democracies for decades, in fits and starts rather than a single smooth decline, and the pattern is now well enough documented that leaders can no longer treat a low trust reading as background noise. A public manager who ignores this trend inherits its costs anyway: heavier enforcement budgets, more contested consultations, lower voluntary compliance, and a public that assumes bad faith before it has heard the argument. A public manager who understands trust as something built through competence, benevolence, and integrity — and spent through their absence — has a lever that budget increases alone cannot buy.

This chapter treats trust and civic engagement as two sides of one relationship. Citizens participate more, and comply more readily, in systems they believe are competent, fair, and honest; and participation itself, done well, is one of the more reliable ways to build that belief. The chapter also confronts a harder truth: the surveys used to track trust are useful but imperfect instruments, and a manager who reads them uncritically can chase the wrong fix.

## Core concepts

**Institutional trust**, sometimes called political trust when it refers specifically to government, is a citizen's willingness to be vulnerable to an institution's actions based on the expectation that it will perform as expected, without the citizen needing to monitor or control it directly (see [Trust (social science)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_(social_science))). It is distinct from **interpersonal trust** between people who know each other and from **generalized social trust**, a diffuse expectation that strangers are trustworthy; the three move together loosely, not in lockstep, and conflating them misdiagnoses the problem. Trust in a specific institution — a police force, a tax authority, a health regulator — can also diverge sharply from trust in 'government' as an abstract, aggregated category, which is why single-item questions about 'the government' are a blunter instrument than they first appear.

The most influential academic account of what makes an institution trustworthy identifies three components: **ability** (or competence — the skill and capacity to do what it says it will do), **benevolence** (perceived to care about citizens' interests, not only its own), and **integrity** (adheres to principles citizens find acceptable and is honest and consistent in applying them). This ability–benevolence–integrity framework originates in organizational trust research by the management scholars Roger Mayer, James Davis, and F. David Schoorman, and has since been widely adapted from workplace settings to citizen–government relationships. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) built a parallel, survey-tested framework for public institutions specifically, organized around two pillars: **competence** (reliability and responsiveness) and **values** (openness, integrity, and fairness). The overlap is not a coincidence — both converge on the same claim, that citizens judge institutions the way they judge people: can this actor do the job, does it have my interests in mind, and does it play straight.

**Measuring trust** is done almost entirely through survey research. The OECD runs a cross-national survey on the drivers of trust in public institutions, asking citizens in dozens of countries about their experience of government reliability, responsiveness, openness, integrity, and fairness, and publishes comparative indicators through its *Government at a Glance* series. The Edelman Trust Barometer, an annual survey run by the public relations firm Edelman (see [Edelman (firm)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edelman_(firm))) since 2000, tracks trust in government alongside business, media, and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) across dozens of countries and is widely cited as a general pattern-setter, even though it is a commercial product rather than an official statistic. National social-attitude surveys and regional instruments such as the Eurobarometer add country-specific detail; none should be read as a precise thermometer, and the limitations are structural rather than a matter of survey design quality. **Social desirability bias** shifts responses toward what a respondent believes is expected of them. **Salience effects** mean a question asked the week after a scandal produces a different answer than the same question asked in a calm month, even though the institution has not changed, which makes trend lines far more informative than any one reading. **Cross-national comparability** is weaker than it looks, since 'trust' does not translate identically across languages and cultures, and citizens in different systems hold different baselines for what government should achieve. **Conflating trust with satisfaction** is a common error: a citizen can be highly satisfied with a fast passport renewal while remaining deeply distrustful of the institution issuing it, because satisfaction is transactional and trust is a broader judgement about motive and character. **Aggregation error** — asking about trust in 'government' as one entity — obscures the fact that trust in the police, the tax authority, and elected politicians can move independently, sometimes in opposite directions, within the same country and year. Treat any single trust score as a noisy signal about a real pattern, not a precise measurement to be optimized directly.

**The long-run decline in institutional trust** across many established democracies since the 1960s and 1970s is one of the most studied patterns in political science, with several reinforcing drivers rather than one cause: **delivery gaps** between what citizens expect government to achieve and what it visibly achieves, which erode the competence leg of trust; **corruption and integrity scandals**, which damage trust in an institution as a whole because citizens generalize from a salient failure to a judgement about character; **economic insecurity and rising inequality**, which correlate closely with falling trust because citizens who feel the system does not work for people like them extend that judgement to the institutions running it; **media fragmentation**, which makes it harder for an institution to hold a reputation across a whole population rather than within one information environment (see Chapter 4.4 — Social Media, Misinformation, and Public Communication, the home chapter for this driver); and **declining associational life** — the erosion of the clubs, unions, congregations, and local associations that used to connect citizens to institutions and to each other — documented by the political scientist Robert D. Putnam's account of declining American civic participation since the mid-twentieth century (see [Bowling Alone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_Alone)). No single driver fully explains the pattern or sits within one manager's control, but each points to a lever: deliver visibly, act with integrity, communicate honestly, and support rather than substitute for the associational infrastructure that used to carry trust.

**Civic engagement beyond voting** is the second half of this chapter's scope. Voting is the most visible form of civic participation, but it is episodic and highly aggregated. **Volunteering** — the freely chosen contribution of time and effort to a cause or community, typically without pay (see [Volunteering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volunteering)) — builds social capital and direct familiarity with how institutions work. **Community organizing** — a structured process by which people who share a problem or a locality build durable collective power to influence decision-makers (see [Community organizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_organizing)) — is a more confrontational, power-building form of engagement, and public institutions sometimes struggle to work with it because it is designed to hold them accountable rather than to support them. **Deliberative forums**, in which citizens get time, balanced evidence, and structured discussion to reach a recommendation rather than simply registering a pre-formed preference, are a third channel; this book's home treatment of citizens' assemblies, participatory budgeting, and co-design sits in the chapter on stakeholders and co-production (see Chapter 1.5 — Stakeholders, Citizens, and Co-Production), and this chapter treats them specifically as trust-building mechanisms rather than re-explaining their design. Together these channels constitute **civic engagement**: individual and collective activity, inside or outside formal politics, addressing issues of public concern (see [Civic engagement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civic_engagement)) — each giving citizens direct, repeated contact with how an institution behaves, a far richer trust signal than an occasional vote or survey question.

**The trust–compliance relationship** is the mechanism that makes all of the above practically consequential. The core finding, developed most influentially by the psychologist and legal scholar Tom R. Tyler (see [Tom R. Tyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_R._Tyler)), is that people comply with rules substantially because they judge the *process* that produced and enforces those rules to be fair, not primarily because they fear the *consequences* of non-compliance. This is the theory of **procedural justice**: fairness judgements rest on whether people were given voice, whether the decision-maker was neutral and consistent, whether they were treated with respect, and whether the decision-maker's motives were trustworthy (see [Procedural justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_justice)). Citizens who judge a process as procedurally fair extend the institution **political legitimacy** — an internalized obligation to obey its decisions independent of the sanction for disobeying them (see [Political legitimacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_legitimacy)) — and legitimacy is one of the cheapest compliance technologies an institution can build, since it needs no continuous surveillance or enforcement to work. The converse matters just as much for a manager's budget: an institution that has lost legitimacy must substitute enforcement for consent, and enforcement is expensive, adversarial, and prone to the kind of visible failure that further erodes the trust it was meant to compensate for. Accountability and transparency mechanisms are among the most direct tools for rebuilding this legitimacy (see Chapter 3.4 — Accountability, Transparency, and Legitimacy, the home chapter for those mechanisms); this chapter treats them through the lens of what they do to compliance behaviour, not their formal design.

## Best practices

1. **Track trust trends, not single readings.** Treat any one survey wave as a noisy signal and act on the direction and speed of movement over several years; a single low or high reading is often driven by a recent event rather than a durable shift.
2. **Disaggregate trust by institution, not just by 'government'.** Measure and report trust separately for the specific agency or service a team runs, because aggregated national figures conceal large, actionable differences between institutions.
3. **Diagnose which leg of trust is weak before designing a response.** A competence problem needs a different fix from a benevolence problem (citizens doubting the institution has their interests at heart) or an integrity problem; a campaign aimed at the wrong leg wastes budget and can deepen cynicism.
4. **Redesign the process before redesigning the message.** When citizens experience a process as arbitrary, disrespectful, or opaque, fix the process — decisions, appeals, how staff treat people — rather than launching a campaign to explain a process citizens already correctly judge unfair.
5. **Give citizens voice in decisions that affect them, and prove it changed something.** Being heard, even when the eventual decision goes against a person, raises perceived fairness and future compliance; close the loop by showing what the input changed (see Chapter 1.5 — Stakeholders, Citizens, and Co-Production for the participation mechanisms themselves).
6. **Treat consistency as a trust asset in its own right.** Apply rules the same way to similar cases and be explicit when a case is genuinely different, since perceived arbitrariness damages trust even when individual decisions are defensible.
7. **Invest in the associational infrastructure that carries trust, not only in your own service.** Support the community organizations, volunteer networks, and civic groups that connect citizens to institutions, rather than assuming trust can be built in isolation from the wider civic fabric.
8. **Match enforcement intensity to legitimacy, not only to risk.** Where legitimacy is high, lighter-touch, cooperative enforcement sustains compliance at lower cost; where legitimacy is low, escalate deliberately as a stopgap, not a first resort, while legitimacy is rebuilt.
9. **Make civic participation channels genuinely accessible, not merely open.** An open invitation to volunteer, organize, or deliberate systematically favours citizens with time, confidence, and prior trust; budget for outreach that reaches people who would not otherwise take part.
10. **Report failures as directly as successes.** Institutions that are visibly honest about their own mistakes recover trust faster than institutions caught minimizing one, because the second failure, of integrity, compounds the first.
11. **Separate short-term satisfaction metrics from long-term trust metrics in performance reporting.** A service can score well on transactional satisfaction while institutional trust keeps falling, and a dashboard tracking only the former will miss the erosion until it becomes an enforcement problem.
12. **Train frontline staff explicitly in procedural justice, not only in service standards.** Front-line encounters are where most citizens form their live judgement of an institution's benevolence and integrity; staff who understand voice, neutrality, respect, and trustworthy motives as a skill set, not just a values statement, generate measurably higher compliance from the people they deal with.

## Questions to discuss with your team

**Which of the three legs of trust — competence, benevolence, or integrity — is actually weak in our case, and how do we know?** Teams often default to a competence narrative ('we need to deliver better') because it is the most comfortable to discuss, even when survey and complaint data point to a benevolence or integrity problem instead. Look at what citizens actually say when they distrust your organization: do they doubt your ability to do the job, doubt that you have their interests at heart, or doubt your honesty? Discuss what evidence you have for your answer, beyond intuition, and what you would need to gather if the evidence is thin.

**Where in our processes are we substituting enforcement for legitimacy, and what would it cost to reverse that?** Identify one process where compliance currently depends heavily on inspection, audit, or penalty rather than on citizens' own sense that the rule is fair and worth following. Discuss what a procedural-justice redesign of that process — more voice, more consistency, clearer explanation of decisions — might look like, and estimate, even roughly, what it currently costs to run on enforcement alone. Weigh that estimate against the cost of the redesign.

**Which of our civic participation channels reach only the people who already trust us?** Volunteering programmes, community panels, and consultation exercises tend to attract people who are already engaged and confident that engagement is worthwhile, which can make participation data look healthy while masking who is absent. Review your last few participation initiatives and discuss who was systematically missing, and what a deliberately resourced effort to reach them, rather than a wider open invitation, would look like. Consider what it would mean for your legitimacy if the people least well served by your organization are also the least represented in how you hear from citizens.

**How do we distinguish a genuine trust problem from a volatile survey reading?** A single bad quarter on a trust indicator can reflect a real, durable shift or a temporary reaction to one news cycle, and treating the two the same way wastes resources or, worse, misses an early warning. Discuss what a multi-year trend in your available data actually shows, and agree in advance what kind of movement — in direction and size — would justify a significant response rather than continued monitoring.

**What would our compliance strategy look like if we assumed legitimacy, rather than deterrence, was doing most of the work?** Many compliance strategies are designed around the assumption that people follow rules mainly because they fear the consequences of breaking them, even though the evidence suggests fair process and perceived legitimacy do more of that work for most citizens most of the time. Pick one area where enforcement costs are rising and discuss what would change in your approach if you treated declining legitimacy, not declining deterrence, as the underlying problem.

**If we lost a significant fraction of public trust tomorrow because of a single visible failure, what is our plan for the next ninety days?** Trust is often spent quickly, in a single scandal or visible failure, and rebuilt slowly, over years, which means the response in the first weeks after a failure disproportionately shapes how long the damage lasts. Discuss whether your organization has a genuine plan for owning a failure honestly and quickly, distinct from a standard communications response, and whether your team has the authority and the instinct to use it under pressure.

## In practice: a public value example

A national customs and excise agency in a mid-sized economy had spent three years watching voluntary compliance among small and medium-sized importers decline, even as it increased the number of physical inspections and doubled the penalty schedule for underdeclared goods. Revenue from penalties rose, but so did the administrative cost of processing disputes, and the agency's own trust survey — a short annual questionnaire it had run for a decade — showed a steady fall in the proportion of importers who agreed the agency 'treats businesses like mine fairly'.

The agency's director of compliance strategy proposed a different diagnosis. Interviews with a sample of importers, run by an external research team to reduce social-desirability bias, found that most importers did not dispute the agency's competence — declarations were processed quickly and the guidance was technically accurate — but distrusted its motives and its consistency. Two businesses with near-identical declarations had received very different treatment from different regional offices, word of which had spread through trade associations faster than any of the agency's own communications. Importers described feeling that the agency assumed bad faith by default and offered little opportunity to explain an honest mistake before a penalty was applied.

Rather than adding a communications campaign explaining the penalty schedule, the agency redesigned the process itself around procedural justice. Regional offices adopted a shared decision protocol to reduce inconsistency between them. Importers flagged for review were given a structured opportunity to explain a discrepancy before, not after, a penalty notice was issued, and officers were trained to document the reasoning behind a decision in plain language the importer would receive. The agency also published, in aggregate and anonymized form, how often reviews led to no action, a reduced penalty, or the original penalty standing, making its own decision pattern visible rather than assumed.

Within two years, the agency's own trust survey showed 'fair treatment' agreement recovering most of its decline, and — more importantly for the agency's budget — the proportion of declarations requiring a formal dispute fell by close to a third, even though the underlying penalty schedule had not been relaxed. The agency's leadership described the result less as a communications success than as evidence that its own inconsistency, not its penalty levels, had been driving the compliance problem the whole time.

This scenario is illustrative and fictional; it does not describe any real customs agency's programme.

## Four sector lenses

### Local government

Local government typically starts from a trust advantage: residents can see roads repaired, bins collected, and planning decisions made, which gives local institutions a shorter, more visible feedback loop between action and citizen judgement than most national bodies enjoy. That same visibility is a double-edged sword, since a single poorly handled planning dispute or service failure travels through a small community fast and can disproportionately damage trust built over years of ordinary competence. The distinctive local craft is using that proximity deliberately — face-to-face procedural justice in planning hearings, benefits decisions, and code enforcement — rather than assuming physical closeness alone generates trust.

### National government

National government institutions operate at a scale and a distance from most citizens that makes competence and integrity signals travel mainly through media coverage rather than direct experience, which is precisely why aggregated national trust indices are both the most widely reported and the least diagnostically useful measure in this chapter's toolkit. The practical challenge is translating a large, impersonal institution's actions into something that reads, to an individual citizen, as fair and consistent treatment; this pushes national bodies toward standardized procedural-justice design — consistent rules, documented reasoning, accessible appeals — as a substitute for the face-to-face trust-building local government can rely on more directly.

### Social sector and nonprofit

Nonprofits and non-governmental organizations often start with higher baseline trust than government, in the Edelman Trust Barometer's cross-national pattern and in most national surveys, because they are perceived as closer to a cause and less encumbered by political motive; but that trust is more fragile at the sector level, since a high-profile scandal at one charity measurably depresses giving and volunteering intentions toward unrelated charities as well (see Chapter 3.8 — Social Sector and Nonprofit Management for the governance structures this sector uses to guard against that spillover). The distinctive task for this sector is protecting a shared reputational commons: individual organizations have a collective, not just individual, stake in sector-wide integrity and transparency norms.

### Multilateral and international

Multilateral and international bodies face the hardest version of this chapter's problem, because they act mainly through member states and rarely have direct, repeated contact with the citizens whose trust they need, which strips away most of the everyday procedural-justice mechanisms — voice, consistent treatment, visible fairness — that build trust at the national or local level. Where these bodies do reach individuals directly, through field operations, humanitarian response, or dispute mechanisms, the procedural-justice literature's findings still apply and are often under-applied, since an international body that treats affected communities as passive recipients rather than as parties owed a fair, explained process forfeits legitimacy it cannot easily rebuild through a member state's intervention on its behalf.

## Common failure modes

**Treating trust as a communications problem.** An organization responds to a falling trust score with a rebranding exercise or a messaging campaign, without examining whether the underlying cause is a competence, benevolence, or integrity failure that no amount of messaging will fix. This not only fails to solve the problem but often deepens it, since citizens who already doubt an institution's honesty read a glossy campaign as further evidence that its priority is appearance rather than substance.

**Chasing the survey instead of the driver.** An organization sets a target for its own trust-index score and manages toward the number — for example, by timing survey administration to avoid a recent controversy — rather than toward the underlying reliability, fairness, and honesty the number is meant to represent. This is a form of Goodhart's law applied to a soft metric (see Chapter 3.11 — Quality, Safety, and Performance Management, which treats this pattern in performance indicators generally): the measure improves while the thing it was meant to track does not.

**Escalating enforcement in response to falling legitimacy.** When voluntary compliance falls, an organization's default response is often to increase inspection, audit, and penalty intensity, which raises short-term enforcement revenue or compliance while further damaging the legitimacy that was driving non-compliance in the first place. This produces a costly cycle in which each round of escalation buys a smaller compliance gain than the last, at a steadily rising enforcement cost (see Chapter 3.10 — Regulation and Public Risk Management for the design choices that determine how much a regulator relies on this pattern).

**Consulting only the already engaged.** An organization runs open civic participation exercises that reach the same confident, well-resourced, already-trusting citizens each time, and mistakes healthy participation numbers for a healthy trust relationship with the wider public. The resulting decisions can entrench, rather than correct, the very inequities that made some groups distrustful of the institution in the first place.

**Confusing satisfaction with trust.** An organization tracks transactional satisfaction scores (was this specific interaction quick and pleasant) and reports them as evidence of institutional health, while a slower-moving decline in trust in the institution's motives and honesty goes undetected until it surfaces as falling compliance or public resistance to a major decision.

## Maturity model

| Dimension | Initiate | Develop | Standardize | Manage | Orchestrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trust measurement | Trust is discussed anecdotally, with no survey or trend data | A trust or satisfaction survey exists but is run inconsistently and not disaggregated by institution | A standard trust survey, aligned to a recognized framework (such as competence and values), is run on a fixed schedule for this organization specifically | Trust trends are reviewed against a defined threshold, with a named owner accountable for responding to sustained decline | Trust data is shared and benchmarked with peer institutions and civil society, and informs cross-organization strategy |
| Procedural fairness in service design | Processes are designed around organizational convenience, with no attention to voice, consistency, or explanation | Some frontline staff apply informal procedural-justice practices, inconsistently and without training | Voice, neutrality, respect, and clear explanation are designed into major decision processes and taught in standard training | Procedural-fairness outcomes (appeal rates, complaint patterns) are measured and used to correct inconsistent processes | Procedural-fairness standards are coordinated with delivery partners and other agencies a citizen encounters in the same case |
| Civic participation infrastructure | No structured channel exists for civic participation beyond statutory minimums | Ad hoc volunteering or consultation opportunities exist without a consistent method | A standing infrastructure (volunteer programme, community panel, deliberative forum) is documented and resourced | Participation reach and representativeness are measured against population data and gaps are corrected | Participation infrastructure is shared across organizations and adapts as communities and issues change |
| Compliance strategy | Compliance strategy assumes deterrence is the only lever and is not reviewed against trust or legitimacy data | Enforcement intensity is set by risk alone, without reference to trust or procedural-fairness evidence | A documented policy matches enforcement intensity to measured legitimacy, distinguishing cooperative from punitive approaches by case type | Compliance costs and legitimacy trends are tracked together, and rising enforcement cost triggers a legitimacy review | Compliance strategy is coordinated across agencies dealing with the same population, avoiding contradictory enforcement signals |
| Response to trust-damaging events | Failures are minimized or managed reactively, with no prepared approach | An informal response exists but is not rehearsed or resourced | A documented rapid-response protocol exists, emphasizing early, honest disclosure | Response effectiveness is measured against trust recovery over time, not just short-term media coverage | Lessons from trust-damaging events are shared across the sector to strengthen collective resilience |

## Checklist

- [ ] Trust trend data (not a single reading) reviewed for this organization specifically, not only at the aggregated 'government' level.
- [ ] Weak leg of trust (competence, benevolence, or integrity) diagnosed from evidence, not assumed.
- [ ] At least one process where enforcement is substituting for legitimacy identified and reviewed for a procedural-justice redesign.
- [ ] Voice, neutrality, respect, and clear explanation checked in the design of any process that imposes a decision on a citizen.
- [ ] Civic participation channels reviewed for who is systematically absent, not just how many people take part.
- [ ] Compliance strategy checked against legitimacy evidence, not deterrence assumptions alone.
- [ ] Frontline staff trained in procedural-justice practice, not only service standards.
- [ ] Satisfaction metrics and trust metrics tracked and reported separately.
- [ ] A rapid, honest disclosure protocol exists and is rehearsed for the next trust-damaging failure.
- [ ] Trust and participation data shared with delivery partners or peer institutions where a citizen's experience spans more than one organization.
