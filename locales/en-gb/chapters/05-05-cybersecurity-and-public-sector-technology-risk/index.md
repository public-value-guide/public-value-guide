# Chapter 5.5 — Cybersecurity and Public Sector Technology Risk

**A cyberattack on a public institution is not an information-technology incident but a direct assault on citizens' safety, entitlements, and trust, and must be resourced, governed, and rehearsed as such.**

## Why this matters in public value

When a hospital's systems are encrypted by an attacker, clinicians cannot see medication histories, ambulances are diverted, and operations are cancelled. When a water utility's control systems are compromised, the public value at stake is not a server outage but the safety of drinking water. When a benefits agency's database is breached, the people most exposed are often the people least able to absorb the consequences: a delayed payment, a stolen identity, a family that cannot prove who it is to the state that owes it support. **[Computer security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_security)**, commonly called cybersecurity, is the practice of protecting computer systems, networks, and data from unauthorised access, disruption, or damage. In public institutions, that practice sits directly on the path between the state and the citizen it serves.

This is why the chapter frames cyber risk as a public value question, not only a technical one. Legitimacy and support depend on citizens believing that the institutions holding their data and running their essential services can be trusted to keep both safe (see Chapter 1.2 — The Strategic Triangle: Legitimacy, Value, and Capacity). A single well-publicised breach can do lasting damage to that trust, in the same way a mishandled emergency does, because citizens judge institutions on how they behave under attack as much as on how they perform day to day (see Chapter 3.12 — Crisis, Emergency, and Resilience Management, which treats cyber incidents as one category within the broader discipline of resilience). Cybersecurity is also inseparable from data governance: an institution cannot protect what it does not know it holds, and the interoperability and openness that create public value from data also widen the surface an attacker can exploit (see Chapter 5.4 — Public Sector Data, Interoperability, and Open Data).

The resourcing problem is acute and specific to the public and social sectors. Large national agencies increasingly have dedicated security teams and enterprise budgets; the water board serving a small town, the regional hospital trust, and the local nonprofit running a domestic-violence shelter typically do not, despite holding data whose sensitivity rivals or exceeds that of any bank. Attackers know this. Under-resourced public bodies are not an edge case in this chapter; they are close to its centre, because the median public institution facing this risk today is not a national ministry but a small local authority, a regional utility, or a charity with a handful of information-technology staff and no security specialist at all.

Finally, this is a live and worsening problem, not a hypothetical one. Ransomware campaigns increasingly target local governments, hospitals, schools, and social-sector organisations precisely because they combine sensitive data, essential services, low tolerance for downtime, and comparatively weak defences — a combination that makes them more likely to pay and less likely to have prevented the attack in the first place. Treating cybersecurity as a specialist add-on rather than a governance-level public value commitment is, for this class of organisation, close to an invitation.

## Core concepts

**[Critical infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_infrastructure)** is the set of systems and assets — energy, water, transport, healthcare, finance, communications — so essential that their incapacity or destruction would have a debilitating effect on safety, the economy, or public confidence. Critical infrastructure protection is the discipline of securing these systems against physical and digital threats alike; it matters here because many of the most consequential public-sector cyber incidents are not attacks on abstract data but attacks on the operational technology that keeps physical infrastructure running, distinct from the information technology that keeps records and processes transactions.

A **threat actor** is any individual, group, or state that may attempt to compromise a system; in the public sector these range from financially motivated criminal groups to state-sponsored actors pursuing espionage or disruption, and the same institution may face both simultaneously with very different objectives. **[Ransomware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransomware)** is malicious software that encrypts an organisation's data or systems and demands payment for their release; it has become the dominant threat to public bodies precisely because essential services cannot tolerate prolonged downtime, which increases the pressure to pay. **[Phishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phishing)** is a form of social engineering in which attackers deceive staff into revealing credentials or installing malware, typically through deceptive email, and remains the most common way an attacker first gains a foothold, because it targets people rather than technology.

A **[data breach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_breach)** is the unauthorised access, disclosure, or loss of information, whether through an external attack, an insider, or simple human error such as a misconfigured database left open to the internet. A **[supply chain attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_chain_attack)** compromises an organisation indirectly, by targeting a less secure supplier, software vendor, or managed-service provider that the target organisation trusts; these are especially dangerous for the public sector because so many public bodies share the same handful of software vendors, meaning one vendor compromise can cascade into hundreds of institutions simultaneously.

**Cyber hygiene** is the set of routine practices — patching software promptly, enforcing strong authentication, limiting who can access what, backing up data and testing the restoration of those backups — that prevent the large majority of successful attacks without requiring exotic tools. Most breaches exploit known, unpatched vulnerabilities or weak credentials rather than novel techniques, so the gap between an organisation's actual cyber hygiene and its stated policy is usually a better predictor of its risk than the sophistication of the threats it names in its risk register.

The **[zero trust architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_trust_architecture)** is a security design principle holding that no user or device should be trusted by default, even inside an organisation's own network, and that every request for access must be verified regardless of where it originates. It has become influential in public-sector guidance because it fits how modern government work actually happens — staff, contractors, and citizens accessing services from many devices and locations — better than an older model that trusted anything already inside the perimeter.

**[Computer security incident management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_security_incident_management)**, usually called incident response, is the organised process of detecting, containing, eradicating, and recovering from a security incident, followed by a review of what allowed it to happen. A **disclosure obligation** is a legal or regulatory duty to notify affected individuals, regulators, or the public when personal data or critical services have been compromised, within a specified time and to a specified standard, on the logic that citizens and oversight bodies have a right to know when their data or their essential services have been put at risk, and that timely disclosure allows people to protect themselves.

## Best practices

1. **Treat cybersecurity as a governing-body responsibility, not a delegated technical function.** Boards, councils, and executive teams should receive regular, plain-language briefings on cyber risk and approve the budget for it directly, rather than leaving it to be negotiated inside a wider information-technology line item where it is the easiest thing to cut.
2. **Inventory what you hold and what depends on it before you decide how to protect it.** An organisation cannot secure data or systems it has not mapped; a current asset and data inventory, cross-referenced against the interoperability and data-governance work covered elsewhere (see Chapter 5.4 — Public Sector Data, Interoperability, and Open Data), is the precondition for every other practice on this list.
3. **Apply the basics everywhere before spending on anything advanced.** Patch management, multi-factor authentication, tested backups, and least-privilege access control stop the overwhelming majority of real-world attacks; a public body without these in place should not be prioritising threat-intelligence subscriptions or bespoke monitoring tools.
4. **Adopt a recognised framework rather than inventing a bespoke one.** Frameworks such as the National Institute of Standards and Technology's Cybersecurity Framework give a shared vocabulary and a structure — identify, protect, detect, respond, recover — that a small organisation can adopt in simplified form and a large one can adopt in full, without reinventing basic taxonomy.
5. **Pool resources across small organisations rather than each buying alone.** A shared security operations service, a regional cyber-incident response pool, or a sector-wide procurement framework can give a group of small local authorities or nonprofits protection that none of them could afford individually — the single most direct answer to the under-resourcing problem this chapter centres on.
6. **Write the incident response plan before the incident, and rehearse it.** A plan drafted for the first time during a live attack is a plan drafted badly, under panic, by people who have never tested whether it actually works; a plan exercised at least annually turns a chaotic first hour into a rehearsed sequence of known steps (see Chapter 3.12 — Crisis, Emergency, and Resilience Management for the wider discipline this draws on).
7. **Decide the disclosure and payment policy in advance, not during the incident.** Whether the organisation will consider paying a ransom, who has authority to make that call, and how and when affected people will be told should be board-approved policy, not an improvised decision made under the pressure of a live extortion demand.
8. **Segment networks so one compromise cannot become every compromise.** Keeping operational technology that controls physical infrastructure separate from general office information-technology, and separating high-sensitivity systems from lower-sensitivity ones, limits how far an intruder who gets in through one weak point can travel.
9. **Manage third-party and supply chain risk as rigorously as internal risk.** Require security assurances, right-to-audit clauses, and incident-notification obligations in contracts with software vendors and managed-service providers, since a growing share of major public-sector incidents originate with a trusted supplier rather than a direct attack.
10. **Invest in people, not only tools.** Regular, realistic training — including simulated phishing exercises — measurably reduces successful social-engineering attacks, and retaining even one or two skilled security staff in a small organisation is usually worth more than an equivalent spend on additional software licences.
11. **Report incidents honestly and use near-misses as free lessons.** A culture that punishes staff for reporting a suspected breach guarantees that the next one is reported late or not at all; the organisations that improve fastest are the ones that treat every near-miss as evidence to learn from rather than a reason to assign blame.
12. **Build cyber resilience into procurement and grant conditions, not just internal policy.** Funders and commissioning bodies can require a baseline level of security practice from the organisations they fund or contract with, extending the reach of good practice to smaller delivery partners who would otherwise have no external pressure to invest in it (see Chapter 3.7 — Public Procurement and Commissioning).

## Questions to discuss with your team

**Do we know what data and systems we actually hold, and which of them would hurt someone if compromised?** Many organisations discover the true extent of their data holdings only during a breach investigation, which is the worst possible time to learn it. Walk through your major services and ask, for each one, what personal or operationally sensitive data it touches and what would happen to a real person if that data were stolen, altered, or made unavailable. If the honest answer is 'we are not sure,' that uncertainty is itself the finding, and closing it is the first item on your agenda. This question matters most for organisations that have grown by acquisition, merger, or accumulated legacy systems, where institutional memory of what exists has thinned.

**If we were hit by ransomware tomorrow, could we restore from backup without paying?** The value of a backup is not that it exists but that it has been tested to actually restore, quickly enough, from a version recent enough to matter. Ask when backups were last tested with a real restoration exercise, not just a completion log, and how long a full restoration would take against how long your organisation can tolerate an outage. If those numbers do not match, you have a resourcing decision to make now, not during an incident. This is also the moment to discuss whether backups themselves are isolated from the network an attacker who reaches your main systems could also reach.

**Who has the authority to decide whether we pay a ransom, and have we ever discussed it before being asked to?** This is one of the highest-stakes decisions an executive team can face, often required within hours and with incomplete information about what the attacker actually has. Deciding the process, the authorising individuals, and the principles in advance — even if the final call still depends on the specifics of the incident — prevents an improvised decision made under extortion pressure. Discuss what your jurisdiction's law and your funders' conditions say about ransom payment, and who you would need to notify.

**Are our smallest partners and suppliers a bigger risk to us than we are to ourselves?** A well-secured central organisation can still be compromised through a poorly secured supplier, contractor, or delivery partner with legitimate access to its systems or data. Map the third parties that can reach your systems or hold your data, and be honest about how much assurance you actually have over their security practices versus how much assurance you assume you have. Decide what minimum standard you will require of them going forward, and how you will verify it rather than simply asking for a written assurance.

**Would we know we had been breached, and how quickly?** Some of the most damaging incidents are not the ones that happen fast but the ones that go undetected for months, during which an attacker has quietly explored, escalated privileges, and exfiltrated data. Discuss how you would actually detect unusual activity today, who is watching for it, and whether that monitoring runs outside office hours, since many attacks are launched deliberately at night or over a holiday weekend when response is slowest. If detection depends entirely on a citizen or a partner organisation telling you something is wrong, that is a critical gap.

**Does our board or governing body understand this risk well enough to govern it, or have we quietly outsourced that judgement to the information-technology team?** Cyber risk decisions involve real trade-offs between cost, service continuity, and citizen protection that are properly the governing body's business, not a delegated technical matter to be nodded through. Discuss how cyber risk is currently reported to your board or council, whether it is in language a non-specialist can actually act on, and whether the budget for it is decided with the same rigour as any other major risk. If your governing body could not currently explain your organisation's top three cyber risks in its own words, that is the gap to close first.

## In practice: a public value example

A mid-sized regional transit authority — fictional, illustrative of a pattern rather than a report of any real organisation — ran ageing scheduling and payment systems that had been patched piecemeal for a decade rather than modernised. Its information-technology team was three people, responsible for everything from help-desk tickets to network security, and cybersecurity had never had its own line in the budget; it lived inside a general 'systems maintenance' allocation that had not grown in real terms for years.

An attacker gained access through a phishing email sent to a finance officer, using a spoofed invoice from a supplier the authority genuinely used. Once inside, the attacker spent six weeks quietly moving through the network before deploying ransomware that encrypted the scheduling system, the fare payment platform, and the authority's backups, which had never been isolated from the main network. Buses kept running on drivers' memory of their routes for the first day, but fares could not be collected electronically, real-time information screens went dark, and the authority's finance systems were unusable for payroll.

The executive team faced the ransom decision with no pre-agreed policy: no one had decided in advance who had authority to approve a payment, what the legal position was, or what they would tell riders. Restoration from backup was not an option, because the backups were encrypted along with everything else. After nine days of disruption, partial service restoration, and a payment the authority's board later described as the least-bad option available at the time, the authority commissioned an independent review.

The review's findings were unglamorous and entirely consistent with the pattern this chapter describes: no multi-factor authentication on finance systems, no network segmentation between office systems and payment infrastructure, no tested backup restoration in over two years, and no board-level visibility into cyber risk at all before the incident. The authority's response was equally unglamorous — a phased plan to fix the basics, a shared security-monitoring arrangement with two neighbouring transit authorities to split the cost of specialist coverage none of them could afford alone, and a standing board agenda item on cyber risk. None of it was expensive by the standards of a major information-technology programme; all of it had been skipped for years because it had never been anyone's clearly assigned job to insist on it.

## Four sector lenses

### Local government

Local authorities sit at an uncomfortable intersection: they hold highly sensitive data — social care records, housing and benefits information, electoral registers — and run services citizens cannot easily do without, but they typically have the smallest security budgets and the least specialist staff of any level of government. A large national ministry can build an in-house security operations centre; a small district council usually cannot, which makes shared services, regional pooling, and national support programmes the practical route to adequate protection rather than an optional extra. Local elected officials are also often the least equipped, of any tier of government, to interrogate a technical risk briefing, which makes plain-language reporting to elected members a distinct governance challenge at this level.

### National government

National governments generally have the resources to build dedicated cybersecurity agencies and national frameworks — bodies such as the United Kingdom's **[National Cyber Security Centre (United Kingdom)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cyber_Security_Centre_(United_Kingdom))** or the United States' **[Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybersecurity_and_Infrastructure_Security_Agency)** exist to set standards, coordinate response, and support less-resourced public bodies within their jurisdiction. The distinctive national-level challenge is less about basic hygiene and more about state-sponsored and highly capable threat actors, the interdependence of critical national infrastructure across sectors and private operators, and the diplomatic and legal dimensions of attribution and response, which rarely feature at the local or organisational level.

### Social sector and nonprofit

Nonprofits and NGOs often hold some of the most sensitive data of any sector — health records, records of survivors of violence or persecution, refugee and asylum casework — while operating on the thinnest security budgets of any organisation type in this book, because funders rarely earmark money for security and grant conditions rarely require it. Many social-sector organisations also depend on volunteers and high staff turnover, which makes consistent security training and access control harder to sustain than in a stable, professionally staffed organisation; a security incident can be existential for a small charity in a way it rarely is for a government department, because reputational damage and loss of donor trust can end the organisation outright (see Chapter 3.8 — Social Sector and Nonprofit Management).

### Multilateral and international

Multilateral and international bodies operate across jurisdictions with different disclosure laws, different definitions of critical infrastructure, and different levels of national cyber capability, which makes coordination itself a distinct problem: an international body's own systems may be well protected while its national member agencies vary enormously, and a body such as the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development or the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity plays as much a standard-setting and capacity-building role as an operational one. The multilateral lens is also where cross-border incident response and information-sharing arrangements matter most, since an attack that begins in one country's infrastructure can cascade into another's within hours, faster than most international coordination mechanisms are built to move.

## Common failure modes

**Security theatre without operational substance.** An organisation adopts an impressive-looking policy document, a compliance certificate, or a single expensive tool, and treats the box as ticked, while basic hygiene — patching, multi-factor authentication, tested backups — remains inconsistent. This costs real money for the appearance of security rather than security itself, and it is often only discovered to have been theatre after an incident exposes the gap between policy and practice.

**The unowned risk.** Cybersecurity is assumed to be someone else's job — the information-technology team's, a vendor's, a regulator's — and no single accountable owner exists at governing-body level. This produces a risk that is discussed everywhere and decided nowhere, and it is precisely the pattern that leaves organisations unable to answer basic questions about their own exposure when asked.

**Underinvestment justified by absence of a prior incident.** Because a well-run security programme produces no visible event when it succeeds, it is easy to defund in a tight budget round in favour of something with a visible output, and the absence of a breach so far is mistaken for evidence of adequate protection rather than evidence that has not yet been tested. This mirrors the same budgetary logic that undermines resilience investment more broadly (see Chapter 3.12 — Crisis, Emergency, and Resilience Management).

**Legacy systems kept running past their supportable life.** Public bodies frequently run software or hardware long after a vendor has stopped issuing security patches for it, because replacement is expensive, disruptive, or politically unpopular, and the system 'still works.' An unpatched, unsupported system is not a legacy inconvenience; it is an open door that costs nothing for an attacker to walk through.

**Blame-first incident culture.** When staff who report a suspected breach or a mistake are disciplined rather than supported, future incidents are reported late, minimised, or not reported at all, and the organisation loses the early-warning signal that timely reporting provides. This failure mode compounds every other one on this list, because none of the fixes above work if the organisation cannot see its own problems honestly.

## Maturity model

| Sub-dimension | Initiate | Develop | Standardise | Manage | Orchestrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Governance and accountability | Cyber risk is not discussed at governing-body level; ownership is unclear | A named owner exists but reports informally and irregularly | Cyber risk is a standing board agenda item with a defined owner and reporting format | Risk is measured against targets, with resourcing decisions tied to the reporting | The organisation shares governance practice and risk intelligence with peer bodies and sector networks |
| Basic hygiene (patching, access control, backups) | Ad hoc; depends on individual staff remembering to act | Documented practice exists but is inconsistently applied across teams | A common standard is mandated and applied organisation-wide | Hygiene compliance is measured, reported, and gaps trigger corrective action | Hygiene standards are coordinated with suppliers and partners as a condition of connection |
| Incident response capability | No written plan; response would be improvised | A plan exists but has never been exercised | A plan is documented, assigned, and rehearsed at least annually | Response performance is measured against defined targets and reviewed after every incident | The organisation participates in shared incident response arrangements across its sector or region |
| Third-party and supply chain risk | Supplier security is not assessed | Some contracts mention security but assurance is not verified | Security requirements are standard contract terms with a verification process | Supplier risk is actively monitored and reviewed on a schedule | Security requirements are coordinated across a shared supplier base with peer organisations |
| Disclosure and transparency | No defined process for notifying affected people or regulators | An informal process exists but timing and ownership are unclear | A documented disclosure process meets legal minimums consistently | Disclosure performance is tracked and the process is improved after each use | The organisation contributes anonymised incident learning back to its sector to help peers improve |

## Checklist

- [ ] A named individual or committee at governing-body level owns cyber risk and reports on it regularly, in plain language.
- [ ] A current inventory exists of what data and systems the organisation holds, and which are most sensitive or most critical to a service.
- [ ] Multi-factor authentication is enforced on all systems that hold sensitive data or control critical services.
- [ ] Backups are taken regularly, stored isolated from the main network, and restoration has been tested within the last twelve months.
- [ ] Software and systems are patched on a defined schedule, and any system that can no longer be patched has a documented plan to replace or isolate it.
- [ ] A written incident response plan exists, names who has authority to make key decisions, and has been rehearsed through a realistic exercise in the last year.
- [ ] A board-approved policy exists on ransom payment and on how and when affected people, regulators, and the public will be notified.
- [ ] Contracts with software vendors and managed-service providers include security assurances and incident-notification obligations.
- [ ] Staff receive regular, realistic security training, including simulated phishing exercises.
- [ ] Reporting a suspected incident or near-miss is treated as valued information, not grounds for blame.
- [ ] Shared or pooled security arrangements have been explored with peer organisations of similar size and budget.
- [ ] Cyber risk features explicitly in the organisation's wider crisis and resilience planning, not as a separate, disconnected plan.
