# Chapter 4.1 — Behavioural Public Policy

**Because people rarely decide the way classical policy models assume, the most cost-effective lever a public body has is often not a new law or a new budget line but a better-designed choice, and the same design power that helps people also carries a real risk of manipulation that only transparency can check.**

## Why this matters in public value

Most public policy is still designed as though citizens read every letter, weigh every option against their stated preferences, and act on the conclusion. Decades of research in psychology and economics show this is not how most decisions actually get made: people rely on mental shortcuts, are swayed by how a choice is presented, and often do today what is easiest rather than what they themselves say they want. A leader who ignores this gap keeps building policies for a citizen who does not exist, and then blames 'low take-up' or 'non-compliance' on the citizen rather than on the design.

Public value theory treats this as a question of operational capacity as much as evidence. A benefits scheme, a tax payment system, or a public health campaign can be legally sound and well funded and still fail to convert intended value into delivered value if the moment of decision is badly designed (see Chapter 1.2 — The Strategic Triangle: Legitimacy, Value, and Capacity). Behavioural public policy is the discipline of closing that gap deliberately, using evidence about how people actually decide rather than how policy documents assume they decide.

The reason this matters more this year than it did a decade ago is that behavioural techniques have moved from a novelty to a default instrument in many governments' toolkits, often bundled into digital services where a single design choice — a pre-ticked box, a reminder text message, a comparison chart — can be deployed to millions of people at almost no marginal cost. That scale is exactly what makes the ethical question urgent: a change too small to notice, applied to an entire population, can shift outcomes considerably, and the same low cost that makes a nudge attractive to a budget-constrained public body also makes it easy to deploy without the scrutiny a law or a spending programme would receive.

Finally, behavioural public policy matters because it sits inside a wider instrument mix rather than replacing it. It is one of the four families of policy instrument a leader can reach for — alongside regulation, spending, and taxation (see Chapter 3.2 — Public Policy and the Policy Cycle) — and treating it as a free-standing discipline, divorced from that wider choice, is how organisations end up using a nudge to avoid a harder decision that a law or a budget line was actually required to fix.

## Core concepts

**Behavioural economics** is the field that studies how psychological, cognitive, and social factors shape economic decisions, in contrast to the classical assumption that people are consistently rational, well informed, and narrowly self-interested (see [Behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics)). Its central empirical finding, developed over five decades, is that deviations from strict rationality are not random noise that cancels out in aggregate; they are systematic, predictable, and therefore something a policy designer can anticipate rather than merely tolerate.

The psychologists **Daniel Kahneman** and **Amos Tversky** built the research programme that made this possible, documenting a long series of **heuristics and biases**: mental shortcuts that work well most of the time but produce predictable errors under specific conditions (see [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman); see [Amos Tversky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amos_Tversky)). The **availability heuristic** leads people to judge a risk as larger when examples of it come easily to mind, which is why a rare but vivid harm (a shark attack, a terrorist incident) is often feared more than a common but unremarkable one (a fall at home). **Anchoring** leads an initial number, even an arbitrary one, to distort a subsequent estimate or negotiation. **Loss aversion** means that losing something is felt roughly twice as painfully as gaining the equivalent amount is felt as pleasurable, which is why how a policy change is framed as a loss or a gain can change how strongly people resist it, independent of its actual content. Kahneman's 2011 book *Thinking, Fast and Slow* popularised a two-system account of this: a fast, intuitive, largely automatic mode of judgement, and a slower, effortful, deliberate mode that checks and overrides the first only when actively engaged (see [Thinking, Fast and Slow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow)). Most everyday decisions, including many decisions about public services, are made by the fast system, which is precisely why the design of the moment of choice matters as much as the content of the information supplied to it. A **cognitive bias**, more broadly, is any systematic pattern of deviation from a rational judgement norm, and the catalogue of documented biases now runs well beyond the two named here (see [Cognitive bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias)).

The economist **Richard Thaler** and the legal scholar **Cass Sunstein** translated this research into a policy tool with their 2008 book *Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness*, which named and popularised **nudge theory**: the idea that any aspect of how a choice is presented — its **choice architecture** — predictably influences the decision made, and that this architecture can therefore be designed deliberately to help people choose what they would, on reflection, choose for themselves, without forbidding any option or materially changing its economic cost (see [Nudge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nudge_theory); see [Richard Thaler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Thaler); see [Cass Sunstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cass_Sunstein)). A nudge, on their definition, must remain easy and cheap to avoid; a policy that closes off an option or substantially raises its cost is regulation or taxation wearing a nudge's clothing, not a nudge itself (see Chapter 3.2 — Public Policy and the Policy Cycle for where nudges sit among the wider instrument families).

**Choice architecture** is the broader design discipline nudges sit inside: the deliberate shaping of the environment in which a decision is made (see [Choice architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choice_architecture)). Its best-known technique is the **default option** — the option a person receives automatically if they do nothing — which matters because inertia and the effort of active choice mean defaults are strikingly persistent even when opting out costs almost nothing; making retirement-savings enrolment the default rather than an active choice, for instance, has repeatedly been found to raise participation far more than an equivalent information campaign. **Framing** is the presentation of logically identical information in different terms — 'ninety per cent survival' against 'ten per cent mortality' — which reliably changes the choice people make even though the underlying facts have not changed. **Social proof** describes people's tendency to copy what others around them appear to be doing, particularly under uncertainty, and is the mechanism behind messages that compare a household's energy use to its neighbours' or state that most people in a district pay their taxes on time. Simplification, timely prompts, and making a desired behaviour easier to complete (fewer steps, a pre-filled form) are further recurring techniques; the Behavioural Insights Team's widely used **EAST framework** compresses the practitioner advice to four words — make a behaviour Easy, Attractive, Social, and Timely.

The institutional pattern that has spread furthest is the **behavioural insights unit**, commonly called a 'nudge unit': a specialist team, usually small, embedded in or advising a government, that applies behavioural science methods — frequently including small-scale randomised trials — to redesign specific interventions and test whether the redesign works before it is scaled (see [Behavioural Insights Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioural_Insights_Team) for the archetype, established within the UK Cabinet Office in 2010 and later part-privatised). The pattern has since been adopted, in varying institutional forms, by national governments, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), and the World Bank, among others, and its methodological signature — testing an intervention against a control group before rolling it out, rather than assuming a plausible-sounding redesign will work — is as important to the pattern as the psychology it draws on (see Chapter 2.4 — Public Sector Econometrics and Programme Evaluation for the evaluation methods this depends on).

The ethical debate around all of this centres on **libertarian paternalism**, Thaler and Sunstein's own name for the philosophy behind nudging: paternalist in that it deliberately steers people toward outcomes the designer believes serve their own interests, libertarian in that it preserves, and is supposed to require, a genuine and low-cost option to choose otherwise (see [Libertarian paternalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarian_paternalism)). Critics have questioned whether this combination is coherent at all: if a default works precisely because most people never notice or exercise the opt-out, the freedom being preserved is formal rather than real, and the same techniques that nudge someone toward saving more for retirement could just as easily nudge them toward a worse outcome, with no clear line the technique itself draws between the two. A second, more recent caveat concerns the evidence base itself: behavioural science, like much of experimental psychology, has been through a **replication crisis** in which a meaningful share of published, widely cited findings have failed to reproduce at the same strength, or at all, when independently retested at scale (see [Replication crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis)). Several celebrated early nudge results have since faced exactly this scrutiny, which does not mean the underlying discipline is unsound, but does mean a leader who cites a single well-known study as settled proof that a specific nudge will work in a specific new context is making a stronger claim than the evidence supports (see Chapter 2.6 — Evidence Synthesis and What Works for how to weigh contested findings responsibly).

## Best practices

1. **Test before you scale, every time.** A plausible-sounding redesign of a form, a letter, or a default is a hypothesis, not a result; run it against a control group at small scale before applying it to an entire population, because behavioural effects are often smaller, larger, or reversed compared with what intuition or a single prior study predicted.
2. **Publish that an intervention is a nudge, and why, before or alongside deploying it.** Disclosure does not have to mean announcing the mechanism to every recipient in the moment, but the policy rationale and evidence base should be publicly available on request, because undisclosed behavioural steering is far harder to distinguish from manipulation than a documented, defensible design choice.
3. **Set the default to the option that best serves the person choosing, not the option cheapest for the organisation to administer.** A default chosen to reduce the public body's own processing costs, rather than to help the citizen, is the clearest case where a nudge shades into a self-serving design decision dressed up as behavioural science.
4. **Keep the opt-out genuinely easy, not merely technically available.** A default that is legally reversible but practically buried behind extra steps, unclear language, or a deliberately unhelpful process fails the libertarian half of libertarian paternalism even if it satisfies the letter of it.
5. **Match the instrument to the scale of the problem.** A nudge is often the cheapest and least coercive first move, but for a problem serious enough to justify regulation or spending, treat the nudge as a complement to those instruments, not a substitute chosen because it is easier to deploy without a legislative fight (see Chapter 3.2 — Public Policy and the Policy Cycle).
6. **Build a standing evaluation capability rather than a one-off pilot team.** A behavioural insights function that runs one successful trial and then disbands loses the institutional memory needed to catch a later intervention that does not replicate the earlier one's success.
7. **Treat a single study, however well known, as a lead rather than a licence.** Check whether a celebrated finding has been independently replicated, and at what effect size, before importing it wholesale into a new policy context, population, or country (see Chapter 2.6 — Evidence Synthesis and What Works).
8. **Pilot across the populations the policy will actually reach, not only the easiest one to recruit.** A nudge validated on a convenience sample of one demographic, one language group, or one income band routinely fails to transfer cleanly to a more diverse population the policy is meant to serve.
9. **Route every behavioural intervention through the same ethical review a data-collection or research project would face.** Testing on citizens without their individual consent is defensible under specific conditions common to routine service improvement, but it is not automatically exempt from the scrutiny research ethics would otherwise require.
10. **Report the size of the effect, not only its statistical significance.** A nudge that is 'statistically significant' but shifts behaviour by a fraction of a percentage point may not justify the administrative cost of deploying and monitoring it at scale; decide the threshold of practical importance in advance.
11. **Sunset and re-test long-running nudges periodically.** A prompt or a default that worked when introduced can lose its effect as people habituate to it, or can start to feel manipulative once its mechanism becomes widely understood, and neither failure mode is visible without deliberately checking again.
12. **Keep a public log of active behavioural interventions and their evidence base.** A citizen, an auditor, or an elected overseer should be able to find out, without a special request, which choices in a public service have been deliberately designed to steer behaviour and on what evidence (see Chapter 3.4 — Accountability, Transparency, and Legitimacy).

## Questions to discuss with your team

**Can we list every default, prompt, or framing choice we currently use to influence citizen behaviour, or would we have to go looking for them?** Many nudges accumulate informally, inside a form redesign or a call-centre script, without ever being labelled as a deliberate behavioural intervention or logged anywhere. Discuss whether your organisation could produce that list today, and if not, agree who is responsible for building it. Consider whether the absence of a list is itself a transparency problem, independent of whether any individual choice on it turns out to be defensible.

**Whose interest does our most-used default actually serve — the citizen's, or ours?** Pick the single default your organisation relies on most heavily, such as a renewal option, a payment method, or a communication channel, and discuss honestly whether it was chosen to help the person subject to it or to reduce your own administrative burden. If the two interests align, say so and move on; if they do not clearly align, discuss what evidence would settle the question and who should be asked to provide it.

**If a journalist or an auditor asked us to justify a specific nudge tomorrow, could we?** Choose one live behavioural intervention and walk through the justification you would give: the evidence it is based on, whether it has been tested in your context, and why the opt-out is genuinely accessible. Discuss what gaps that exercise exposes, and whether those gaps would survive real external scrutiny rather than only internal sign-off (see Chapter 3.4 — Accountability, Transparency, and Legitimacy).

**Have we ever cited a famous behavioural finding as settled evidence without checking whether it has replicated?** Ask the team to recall the behavioural research most often invoked in your organisation's own justifications, then discuss honestly whether anyone has checked its replication status recently, rather than assuming a well-known result remains as strong as when it was first published. Consider what a policy of checking replication status before citing a study would cost in time, and weigh that against the cost of building a programme on a finding that later collapses under scrutiny.

**Are we using a nudge because it is the right instrument for this problem, or because it is easier to deploy than the alternative?** Take a policy problem your organisation is currently addressing mainly through information or behavioural design and discuss honestly whether regulation or spending was ever seriously considered and set aside, or simply never raised. Agree what evidence of scale or harm would trigger a genuine reconsideration of the instrument mix, not only of the nudge's own design (see Chapter 3.2 — Public Policy and the Policy Cycle).

**Do we test our interventions on a population that actually resembles the people the policy is meant to reach?** Review the last one or two behavioural trials your organisation ran and discuss who was actually recruited into them, compared with the full population the resulting policy will apply to. Consider what it would take to test across a more representative group before the next rollout, and whether your organisation currently has the capacity, patience, and mandate to do that rather than moving straight from a promising pilot to full deployment.

## In practice: a public value example

A regional transport authority serving a mid-sized metropolitan area had spent several years trying to reduce the number of drivers who let their vehicle emissions certificates lapse before renewal, a compliance gap that both undermined air-quality enforcement and generated a steady stream of avoidable penalty notices and appeals. Analysis showed the problem was rarely deliberate evasion; most lapsed certificates belonged to drivers who intended to renew but let the paper reminder sit in a drawer past its date, then faced a fine that felt disproportionate to an honest lapse.

The authority's newly formed behavioural insights unit, a team of four analysts drawn from its existing data and communications staff, proposed testing three low-cost redesigns against the existing renewal letter: a text message reminder sent ten days before expiry, a version of the letter that reframed the fine in loss terms ('you will lose your no-penalty record' rather than 'a fine may apply'), and a version that made renewal the assumed action by pre-filling an online renewal form with the driver's existing details, requiring only a confirmation click rather than a full re-entry. Rather than rolling out whichever redesign seemed most persuasive on paper, the unit ran all three against a control group receiving the existing letter, across a large enough sample, drawn from across the authority's full service area rather than only its most digitally engaged districts, to detect a meaningful difference.

The results were mixed in an instructive way. The reframed letter produced almost no measurable improvement over the original, which the unit flagged as a caution against assuming every plausible-sounding behavioural technique will work in every context. The text message reminder produced a modest but real reduction in lapses. The pre-filled renewal form produced the largest effect by a wide margin, cutting lapse rates by roughly a third, evidently because it removed effort at the exact moment of decision rather than relying on the driver to remember and act independently.

Before scaling the pre-filled form authority-wide, the unit published a short public note explaining the change, the evidence behind it, and how a driver could still choose to renew by post or in person if they preferred not to use the online form, and it set a date, eighteen months out, to re-test the effect to check it had not faded as drivers grew used to receiving it. The authority also decided, after internal debate, not to extend the same pre-filling technique to a separate scheme offering a discretionary low-emission vehicle subsidy, judging that a financial application involving a genuine choice between options deserved active engagement from the applicant rather than a default that might be accepted without full consideration. This scenario is illustrative and fictional; it does not describe any real transport authority's programme or decision.

## Four sector lenses

### Local government

Local government usually runs the most direct, highest-volume behavioural interventions in the system, because it owns the everyday transactional moments — renewals, waste collection, local tax payment, appointment scheduling — where a citizen actually encounters a choice architecture. Its characteristic constraint is capacity rather than opportunity: a local authority is unlikely to have Kahneman-level in-house expertise, so its strongest move is usually partnering with a national behavioural insights unit, a university research group, or a shared regional service, rather than attempting to build sophisticated trial infrastructure alone for a single service.

### National government

National government is where the behavioural insights unit as an institutional pattern first took recognisable shape, and it is the level with the scale and data infrastructure to run genuinely powered randomised trials across large, diverse populations. Its characteristic risk runs the other way from local government's: with a specialist unit and an evidence base of prior successes on hand, national policy can start reaching for a nudge as the default answer to problems that a nudge cannot actually solve on its own, using an evidently evidence-based intervention to avoid the harder political work of legislating or funding a structural fix (see Chapter 3.2 — Public Policy and the Policy Cycle).

### Social sector and nonprofit

Nonprofits and charities apply many of the same techniques — default donation amounts, social-proof messaging about how many others have already given, simplified sign-up forms — in fundraising and service take-up, often without the trial infrastructure a government unit can afford, and sometimes working with beneficiaries who are more vulnerable to subtle pressure than the general population the technique was originally tested on (see Chapter 3.8 — Social Sector and Nonprofit Management). Their distinctive obligation is proportionate caution: a resource-constrained organisation cannot always run its own randomised trial before deploying a technique, which makes leaning on independently replicated, published evidence, rather than on a single persuasive case study, more important here than almost anywhere else in the book.

### Multilateral and international

Multilateral and international bodies, including the World Bank's own behavioural science work and the OECD's cross-country behavioural insights network, mostly operate as evidence aggregators and standard-setters rather than as direct deployers of nudges to individual citizens. Their distinctive challenge is transferability across radically different administrative, cultural, and economic contexts: a default or a framing validated in one country's population offers, at best, a hypothesis worth testing elsewhere, not a proven result, and an international body's greatest contribution is often building the shared evidence base and ethical guidance that lets a national or local government test responsibly rather than import blindly.

## Common failure modes

**Nudge as a substitute for a harder fix.** A public body reaches for a reminder letter or a redesigned form to address a problem whose real cause is inadequate capacity, an unaffordable fee, or a genuinely difficult access barrier, because the nudge is cheaper and faster to deploy than the regulation or spending that would actually solve it. The measured effect is real but small, the underlying problem persists, and the organisation can point to 'having done something evidence-based' as cover for not having done the harder thing.

**Undisclosed manipulation.** A behavioural intervention is deployed without any public account of what it is or why, on the theory that disclosure would blunt its effectiveness. This treats effectiveness as more important than legitimacy, and it is precisely the pattern that critics of libertarian paternalism point to when they argue the philosophy collapses into ordinary manipulation once the citizen cannot find out what has been done to their choice environment (see Chapter 3.4 — Accountability, Transparency, and Legitimacy).

**Citing a landmark study as settled fact.** An organisation builds a policy on a single well-known behavioural finding — often one popularised in a bestselling book rather than checked against the underlying literature — without asking whether it has since been independently replicated or how large its effect size actually was. When the intervention underperforms the cited study by a wide margin, or fails to replicate at all, the organisation has no fallback because it never treated the original finding as provisional.

**Assuming a nudge transfers automatically across populations.** A technique validated on one demographic, language group, or national population is rolled out to a materially different one without re-testing, on the assumption that a documented psychological mechanism must work the same way everywhere. Effects that depend on specific cultural norms, literacy levels, or trust in the messenger frequently do not transfer, and the failure is often discovered only after full-scale deployment rather than during a pilot.

**Letting a working nudge run unmonitored indefinitely.** An intervention proven effective at launch is left in place for years without re-testing, on the assumption that an initial positive result guarantees a permanent one. Habituation, changing context, or growing public awareness of the mechanism can erode or reverse the original effect, and an organisation that never checks again has no way of knowing whether it is still working, has stopped working, or has started to feel like manipulation to the people subject to it.

## Maturity model

| Dimension | Initiate | Develop | Standardise | Manage | Orchestrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Testing before scaling | Redesigns are deployed on intuition, with no control group or measurement | Some interventions are piloted informally, inconsistently, before wider rollout | A documented standard requires a controlled trial before any behavioural intervention scales | Trial results are tracked against outcomes and reviewed on a fixed schedule, with underperforming interventions withdrawn | Trial infrastructure and findings are shared with other public bodies, avoiding duplicated testing of the same technique |
| Transparency and disclosure | Behavioural interventions are undocumented and undiscoverable by citizens or auditors | An internal record exists but is not published or systematically maintained | A public log of active interventions and their evidence base is maintained and kept current | Disclosure practice is audited against the standard, with gaps treated as a compliance issue | Disclosure practice is benchmarked against and coordinated with peer organisations' transparency standards |
| Evidence quality assurance | Findings from any source, including single studies or popular books, are treated as directly applicable | Some staff informally check replication status, without a consistent process | A standard step requires checking replication and effect size before adopting an external finding | Evidence quality is tracked, with a maintained internal register of which techniques have proven reliable in this organisation's own context | The organisation contributes its own replication findings back to the wider evidence base, strengthening the field beyond its own walls |
| Fit with the wider instrument mix | Behavioural interventions are chosen without reference to regulation or spending alternatives | The instrument choice is occasionally, informally compared with other options | A standard step compares a nudge against regulation, spending, and taxation before a behavioural approach is adopted for a serious problem | The chosen instrument mix is reviewed against outcomes achieved, with a nudge-only response to a large problem flagged for escalation | Instrument-mix decisions, including the choice to nudge, are coordinated with other levels of government or delivery partners addressing the same problem |

## Checklist

- [ ] Every active default, prompt, or framing choice designed to influence citizen behaviour identified and logged.
- [ ] Each logged intervention tested against a control group, at representative scale, before full deployment.
- [ ] Public-facing rationale and evidence base for each intervention available on request or published proactively.
- [ ] Opt-out from every default confirmed to be genuinely easy, not merely technically available.
- [ ] Default option checked to confirm it serves the citizen's interest, not only the organisation's administrative convenience.
- [ ] Cited behavioural findings checked for independent replication and effect size before being relied on.
- [ ] Instrument choice compared against regulation, spending, and taxation before a nudge is used for a serious or high-stakes problem.
- [ ] Pilot populations checked for representativeness against the full population the policy will reach.
- [ ] Each active intervention given a re-test date to check whether its effect has faded or its perception has shifted.
- [ ] Ethical review applied to behavioural trials on citizens, equivalent to the scrutiny a research project would face.
