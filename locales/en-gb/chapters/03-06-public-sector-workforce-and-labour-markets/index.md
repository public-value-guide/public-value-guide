# Chapter 3.6 — Public Sector Workforce and Labour Markets

**A government's strategy is only as good as the employment relationships that carry it out, and those relationships are shaped by a labour market that behaves differently from a private one in ways every public leader needs to name, not assume away.**

## Why this matters in public value

Every public value proposition, however well argued, is delivered by someone: a caseworker, an engineer, a nurse, a planning officer, a procurement specialist. The operational capacity leg of the strategic triangle (see Chapter 1.2 — The Strategic Triangle: Legitimacy, Value, and Capacity) is, in practice, mostly a workforce question — whether the organisation has the right people, in the right numbers, with the right skills, willing to stay long enough to build the institutional knowledge a complex mission requires. A finance ministry can approve every budget line a service needs and still watch delivery fail because the specialist engineers or data analysts it needs will not work for what it can legally pay.

This is not a generic human-resources problem imported into government; the public-sector labour market has structural features a private employer rarely faces. Pay is set, at least in part, through a political process that is visible to voters and opposition politicians in a way a private salary review never is. In many trades and professions, one employer — the state — is the largest or only buyer of a particular kind of labour in a given area, which changes the basic economics of the relationship. And a substantial share of public and social-sector employees report that they are motivated by more than pay, a documented pattern that public leaders can either draw on responsibly or quietly exploit.

Getting the workforce question wrong costs more than money. A civil service that recruits and promotes on patronage rather than merit forfeits the legitimacy that comes from being seen to act impartially. A pay system that ignores market reality haemorrhages the specialist skills a modern public body cannot function without. A workforce plan that ignores demographic change discovers its capability gap only when an entire generation of institutional memory retires within a few years of each other. This chapter sets out the concepts and choices that determine whether an organisation's people are an asset it has actively built or a risk it has passively inherited.

## Core concepts

A **civil service** is the body of permanent, professional officials who staff government administration and continue in post across changes of elected leadership (see [Civil service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_service)). This chapter treats the civil service as a workforce — how it is recruited, paid, organised, and planned for — building on Chapter 3.1's treatment of the civil service as an institutional system within the wider typology of public administration paradigms (see Chapter 3.1 — Public Administration Systems and Models, which is the home chapter for Max Weber's model of bureaucratic organisation and its successors).

Civil service recruitment and career structures fall, broadly, into two families, and most real systems are hybrids of the two. A **career-based system** recruits mainly at entry level, typically straight from education, and expects most employees to spend a working lifetime inside the service, rising through a defined hierarchy; France's *grands corps*, Japan's national civil service, and the United Kingdom's traditional fast stream are commonly cited examples. A **position-based system** recruits separately for each vacant post, at any level, from inside or outside government, evaluating candidates against the specific job rather than a generalist career track; the United States federal government and several Nordic and Anglophone reformers of the 1980s and 1990s lean further toward this model. Career-based systems build deep institutional loyalty but can be slow to bring in scarce external skills; position-based systems recruit specialist talent more readily but can weaken shared identity and complicate succession planning. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) tracks this distinction across its member countries, and most systems today mix career-track generalist roles with position-based specialist hiring for skills such as digital delivery or actuarial analysis.

Both models are usually built on a **merit system**: the principle that recruitment and promotion in public employment should be decided on ability to do the job, assessed through open and fair competition, rather than political connection (see [Merit system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merit_system)). Merit systems were built, historically, in reaction to the **spoils system**, in which a newly elected party filled government posts with its own supporters regardless of competence (see [Spoils system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoils_system)). The United States moved away from wholesale patronage with the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883, which established competitive examination for many federal posts (see [Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendleton_Civil_Service_Reform_Act)); the United Kingdom's equivalent turning point was the Northcote–Trevelyan Report of 1854, which recommended recruitment by open examination and promotion by achievement rather than patronage (see [Northcote–Trevelyan Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northcote%E2%80%93Trevelyan_Report)). Both reforms remain touchstones because the tension they addressed — political control of appointments versus an impartial professional service — resurfaces whenever a newly elected government wants its own people in post quickly.

Public-sector pay is set through a distinctive combination of administrative pay-setting bodies, national or sectoral **collective bargaining** — negotiation between an employer and organised groups of workers, typically trade unions, over pay and conditions (see [Collective bargaining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_bargaining)) — and, in many countries, independent pay review processes designed to keep sensitive pay decisions at arm's length from ministers. A **trade union**, an organisation of workers formed to protect their shared interests in pay, conditions, and job security, is often a stronger, more institutionally embedded actor in the public sector than in much private industry, partly because public-sector unionisation rates have held up better than private-sector rates in most advanced economies (see [Trade union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_union)). The International Labour Organization (ILO), the United Nations agency that sets and monitors international labour standards, addresses this in its Labour Relations (Public Service) Convention, 1978 (Convention No. 151), which calls on states to protect public employees' right to organise and to provide fair procedures for determining their conditions of employment (see [International Labour Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Labour_Organization) and [Labour Relations (Public Service) Convention, 1978](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Relations_(Public_Service)_Convention,_1978)).

Government's position as employer also has a distinctive market structure. A **monopsony** is a market in which a single buyer accounts for most or all of the demand for a good or service, giving that buyer power to influence the price below what a competitive market would set (see [Monopsony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopsony)). Local and national government is often the monopsony or near-monopsony employer for occupations such as teaching, nursing, firefighting, and social work in a given area, which gives public-sector wage-setting more power to depress pay below its competitive level than most private employers have, but also more responsibility for the consequences, since there may be nowhere else locally for a qualified worker in that field to go. Layered on top is political visibility: a public-sector pay award, unlike a private payroll decision, is typically debated in a legislature and treated by government and opposition alike as a signal about fiscal discipline, making pay decisions slower and more constrained by non-labour-market considerations than comparable private employment (see Chapter 3.3 — Public Finance and Budgeting for how the pay bill sits inside the wider budget process).

**Public service motivation (PSM)** is the documented tendency of many people working in government and non-governmental organisations to be motivated, at least in part, by a desire to serve the public interest, rather than by pay and career incentives alone (see [Public service motivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_service_motivation)). The term was coined by the American public-administration scholars James L. Perry and Lois Recascino Wise in a 1990 article setting out rational, norm-based, and affective motives for public service, and it has since become one of the most extensively tested constructs in public-administration research. PSM matters here because it changes what 'getting the incentives right' means: an employer that assumes staff respond only to financial reward will misdesign recruitment (missing candidates drawn to mission) and reward systems (introducing performance pay that research on motivation 'crowding out' suggests can, in some settings, weaken intrinsically motivated effort rather than strengthen it).

**Workforce planning** is the discipline of forecasting an organisation's future staffing needs — by number, location, and skill — against its likely future supply of people, and closing the gap deliberately rather than reactively. In the public sector this must account for demographic ageing concentrated in specific professions (engineering, healthcare, and inspection roles recruited in postwar expansion waves are a recurring example across OECD countries), skills shifts driven by digital service delivery, and pay or hiring freezes imposed for fiscal reasons that can leave a gap unfilled for years. The social sector adds a layer the civil service rarely manages: nonprofit and community organisations often run a **blended workforce** of paid staff and volunteers — people who freely give time and effort without expecting payment (see [Volunteering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volunteering)) — planned alongside each other, which connects workforce planning to the treatment of citizens and volunteers as co-producers of public value, not simply an audience for services (see Chapter 1.5 — Stakeholders, Citizens, and Co-Production).

## Best practices

1. **Decide, deliberately, whether a role belongs on a career track or a position-based track.** State explicitly which roles need deep institutional continuity and which need specialist skills an internal pipeline will not reliably supply.
2. **Publish the recruitment principle you actually follow, not the one you assume everyone follows.** Merit, fairness, and open competition are worth little if hiring managers, panels, and political appointees are not all working from the same published rule set.
3. **Treat the pay review process as a source of legitimacy, not an obstacle to be managed.** A properly resourced, independent pay-setting mechanism protects the workforce from arbitrary freezes and protects ministers from the accusation of buying votes with the payroll.
4. **Negotiate collective bargaining in multi-year cycles wherever the fiscal framework allows it.** Single-year, adversarial rounds consume management attention and erode trust; a framework revisited rather than relitigated each year gives both sides planning certainty.
5. **Know where your organisation is the monopsony employer, and price that responsibility into pay decisions.** If your pay award effectively is the labour market for a profession in your area, underpaying does not just save money; it can shrink the entire local supply of that skill.
6. **Design reward systems around what actually motivates your workforce, not a generic assumption about incentives.** Where public service motivation is strong, invest in mission clarity and autonomy before reaching for performance bonuses, which research on motivation 'crowding out' suggests can sometimes weaken the very effort you intend to reward.
7. **Run a rolling workforce plan, not a one-off headcount exercise.** Track the age profile, skills mix, and attrition pattern of your critical professions at least every two to three years, and treat a looming retirement wave as a fiscal risk, not a surprise.
8. **Fund and staff volunteer coordination as seriously as paid recruitment, wherever a blended workforce is part of the model.** Volunteer management — screening, training, scheduling, recognition — is real professional work; treating it as an afterthought is the most common reason volunteer-dependent programmes under-deliver.
9. **Protect the political neutrality of civil service appointments as a discipline distinct from workforce planning.** Keep the process for filling permanent posts visibly separate from appointing political advisers, so a change of government does not trigger an informal spoils-system reversion.
10. **Audit pay equity by role, not only by headline averages.** Aggregate figures can hide systematic under-payment of specific occupations or groups that an organisation-wide average will mask.
11. **Give managers real, resourced authority over recruitment and retention, not just a headcount target.** A workforce plan that sets numbers centrally but starves frontline managers of hiring budget or decision rights will be worked around rather than followed.
12. **Tell the workforce, in plain terms, what the organisation can and cannot promise on pay, security, and progression.** Ambiguity erodes trust faster than an honest, early statement of an unwelcome constraint.

## Questions to discuss with your team

**Do we actually know whether our recruitment system is career-based, position-based, or an undecided mixture of both?** Many organisations inherit a hybrid system without anyone having chosen it deliberately, which shows up as inconsistent rules for lateral entry, unclear promotion expectations, and friction between long-serving staff and specialist external hires. Walk through your last ten senior appointments and classify each as internally promoted or externally recruited. Discuss whether the pattern you see matches the pattern you actually want, and if not, what would need to change in your recruitment policy to close the gap.

**Where are we the monopsony employer for a profession, and are we pricing that responsibility correctly?** Identify the roles in your organisation for which you are the primary or only local employer of that skill, then compare your pay offer against the best available regional benchmark. Discuss what you know about vacancy rates and time-to-fill for those roles, since a persistent gap is a strong signal that your pay has fallen below the level needed to sustain the local supply of that skill. Consider what it would cost to close the gap against what it is currently costing you in vacancies, overtime, and agency cover.

**Are we relying on public service motivation as a substitute for adequate pay and conditions?** Public service motivation is real and valuable, but it is not infinite, and an organisation that quietly treats staff commitment as a free resource to be drawn on indefinitely eventually burns through it. Discuss honestly whether your current staffing model would survive if your most mission-driven employees decided their commitment had been taken for granted once too often. Identify one concrete change — in workload, recognition, or autonomy — that would show the organisation values that motivation rather than exploiting it.

**What does our workforce look like in ten years if nothing changes about how we recruit today?** Project the age profile of your critical technical and professional roles forward a decade using current retirement patterns, and be specific about which functions face the steepest capability cliff. Discuss whether your current hiring pipeline, apprenticeship or graduate scheme, and knowledge-transfer practice are sized to replace what will be lost, or whether you are planning to discover the gap only once it opens. Where the answer is uncomfortable, decide what you can realistically start now rather than in the next budget cycle.

**If we depend on volunteers alongside paid staff, are we resourcing that relationship as seriously as paid staffing?** List every programme in your organisation that would not function without volunteer effort, and check whether each one has a funded, trained coordinator responsible for it, or whether volunteer management is being absorbed informally into paid staff's existing workload. Discuss what would happen to service delivery if volunteer numbers fell by a third within a year, a pattern not uncommon after a period of economic stress or public health disruption. Consider whether your organisation treats volunteers as co-producers with a genuine stake in the work (see Chapter 1.5 — Stakeholders, Citizens, and Co-Production) or as an unmanaged supplement to headcount.

**Is our approach to collective bargaining building a durable working relationship, or refighting the same battle every round?** Review your last two or three bargaining cycles and note whether they were resolved through genuine negotiation, through a unilateral offer accepted under pressure, or through disruption such as a strike or work-to-rule. Discuss what a multi-year framework agreement would need to contain to be credible to both sides, and what would have to change in how your organisation prepares for bargaining — earlier engagement, better data, a mandate that survives political turnover — to get there. Weigh the cost of that investment against the recurring cost of annual confrontation.

## In practice: a public value example

A national environmental protection agency in a mid-sized country had spent two decades recruiting almost entirely through a classic career-based civil service track: graduates joined as junior inspectors, trained internally, and rose through a defined grade structure over fifteen to twenty years. The model had produced a workforce with deep institutional knowledge of the country's water systems and a strong shared professional identity. It had also produced a workforce whose median age among senior water engineers was fifty-eight, with almost no lateral recruitment of the modelling and remote-sensing specialists the agency now needed to meet new climate-monitoring obligations.

The agency's workforce planning unit modelled the risk starkly: on current retirement patterns, close to half of the agency's most experienced water engineers would leave within six years, and the graduate pipeline that had reliably replaced retirees in the past could not supply the specific digital and environmental-modelling skills the agency's new statutory duties required, because those skills were rarely taught inside the generalist entry route the agency had always used. Recruiting externally for senior specialist posts would mean setting pay above what existing internally promoted staff at the same grade were earning, a step the agency's leadership had avoided for years for fear of internal grievance and public criticism of 'unequal pay for equal grade'.

The agency's director resolved the tension by making the decision explicit rather than avoiding it. She created a separate, clearly labelled specialist track, position-based rather than career-based, with its own pay band justified publicly by reference to the external labour market for environmental data scientists, while leaving the existing career-based inspector track untouched and equally respected. She paired this with a two-year knowledge-transfer requirement: every specialist hired onto the new track was contracted to co-deliver at least two major projects with a retiring senior engineer before that engineer's departure, turning succession planning into a funded, scheduled activity rather than a hope. The change was negotiated with the relevant public-sector union over eight months, with the union's agreement secured in exchange for a commitment that the specialist track would not be used to bypass promotion opportunities on the existing inspector track.

Three years later, the agency had filled the specialist posts it needed, retained more of its retiring cohort as part-time mentors than its previous ad hoc arrangements had achieved, and reported — in an internal review it later made public — that morale on the existing inspector track had held steady rather than declined, which its leadership attributed directly to having negotiated the change openly rather than announcing it as a fait accompli. This scenario is illustrative and fictional; it does not describe any real environmental agency's workforce programme.

## Four sector lenses

### Local government

Local government typically competes for the same professions — planners, engineers, social workers, environmental health officers — against national agencies and the private sector, often with less pay flexibility and a smaller catchment area, which makes the monopsony dynamic in this chapter especially acute for specialist roles in smaller or remote authorities. Collective bargaining is often more fragmented here too, with pay set locally, regionally, or through a national framework local employers only partly control, leaving local leaders accountable for outcomes they did not fully set.

### National government

National government is the traditional home of the classic career-based civil service and the most politically visible pay-setting, since a national pay award is routinely reported, debated in the legislature, and used by government and opposition alike as a signal of fiscal seriousness. This visibility gives national civil services the strongest incentive to use independent pay review mechanisms and clearly published merit-based recruitment principles, since the reputational cost of a perceived return to patronage is highest where public trust in impartial administration is most directly at stake (see Chapter 3.4 — Accountability, Transparency, and Legitimacy).

### Social sector and nonprofit

Social-sector and nonprofit organisations manage the blended paid-and-volunteer workforce this chapter treats as distinctive, and public service motivation is often explicit in how they recruit and describe their mission — a genuine strength, but also a real exposure if it becomes an excuse for chronically low pay (see Chapter 3.8 — Social Sector and Nonprofit Management for the funding and governance structures shaping what these organisations can offer staff). Workforce planning here must also model volunteer supply, which is sensitive to economic conditions and public attention, alongside paid staffing — a forecasting problem most civil service planning tools were never built to handle.

### Multilateral and international

Multilateral and international bodies draw staff from many national civil services and the private sector simultaneously, so career-based and position-based logics coexist within one organisation, often producing the kind of internal tension the fictional agency above had to resolve deliberately. Pay determination is further complicated by the need for internationally comparable, cost-of-living-adjusted scales across many duty stations, and collective bargaining, where it exists, operates across staff associations representing dozens of nationalities rather than one national labour movement, making a multi-year framework agreement both harder to negotiate and more valuable when achieved.

## Common failure modes

**Pay compression under political pressure.** Ministers or boards hold down senior and specialist pay for fear of headline criticism about 'fat cat' salaries, while junior pay drifts upward through incremental settlements, until the internal-versus-external pay gap grows large enough to drive an exodus of exactly the experienced staff the organisation can least afford to lose. The cost shows up gradually, as a hollowing-out of senior technical capacity, rather than as a single visible crisis.

**Treating public service motivation as a free resource.** An organisation leans on staff commitment to mission to justify chronic understaffing or below-market pay, reasoning that people who care about the work will absorb the strain. This works until it does not: burnout and attrition remove the most motivated staff first, since they are the ones most likely to have other options and the least willing to watch an under-resourced mission continue indefinitely.

**Undeclared drift from career-based to position-based recruitment.** An organisation built around internal promotion begins hiring laterally for convenience, without redesigning its promotion rules or pay structure to match, producing two workforces operating under inconsistent expectations within one institution. Long-serving staff perceive, often correctly, that the rules changed without being renegotiated, a reliable driver of grievance even when each hiring decision was individually defensible.

**Collective bargaining conducted as annual crisis management.** An employer negotiates pay one year at a time, under fiscal pressure, with no multi-year framework and minimal preparation, so each round becomes an adversarial contest rather than a negotiation between parties with a continuing relationship. This consumes disproportionate management time and increases the likelihood of disruptive action such as strikes, which then makes the next round more adversarial still.

**Workforce planning that only counts heads, not skills or timing.** An organisation tracks total headcount against budget but does not model the age profile, skill mix, or attrition pattern within it, so a demographic cliff in a critical profession is invisible in the aggregate numbers until it arrives, by which point the lead time needed to train or recruit a replacement has usually already been lost.

## Maturity model

| Dimension | Initiate | Develop | Standardise | Manage | Orchestrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recruitment system design | Career-based and position-based hiring coexist by accident, with no stated policy | A written policy exists but is applied inconsistently across units | A documented, organisation-wide rule sets out which roles use which track, and why | Recruitment outcomes by track are monitored, with gaps triggering a policy review | Recruitment design is benchmarked and adjusted jointly with peer public bodies facing the same skills market |
| Pay determination and bargaining | Pay decisions are made ad hoc, with no defined process or bargaining partner | A bargaining process exists but each round is treated as a one-off | A multi-year pay and bargaining framework is documented and used as standard | Pay outcomes are tracked against market and retention data, with awards adjusted accordingly | Pay strategy is coordinated across public bodies competing for the same regional or sectoral labour pool |
| Motivation and reward alignment | Reward design assumes financial incentive is the only driver of effort | Some attention is paid to non-financial motivation, informally and inconsistently | Reward and recognition schemes are explicitly designed around evidence on public service motivation | Motivation and engagement are measured and used to adjust reward design over time | Motivation strategy is shared and benchmarked with comparable organisations, adapting as the workforce's expectations change |
| Workforce and succession planning | No forward workforce plan exists; gaps are discovered when a post cannot be filled | An informal headcount forecast exists but does not model skills or age profile | A documented workforce plan, covering skills and demographics, is refreshed on a fixed cycle | Workforce plan accuracy is tracked against outcomes, and corrective recruitment or training is funded | Workforce planning is coordinated with education and training providers to shape the future supply pipeline |
| Blended paid-volunteer workforce management | Volunteer effort is used without coordination or a named owner | A volunteer coordinator role exists but is under-resourced relative to the programmes it supports | Volunteer recruitment, training, and recognition follow a documented standard alongside paid staffing | Volunteer capacity and attrition are tracked as a planning input, not an afterthought | Volunteer and paid workforce planning are integrated into a single strategy, developed with volunteers as active participants |

## Checklist

- [ ] Recruitment policy states, explicitly, which roles are career-based and which are position-based, and why.
- [ ] Merit-based recruitment principles are published and applied consistently, with political appointments kept visibly separate.
- [ ] Pay determination process, including any independent review mechanism, is documented and understood by staff.
- [ ] Roles where the organisation is the monopsony employer are identified, with pay benchmarked against the best available regional data.
- [ ] Reward and recognition design reflects evidence on public service motivation, not an assumption that pay is the only lever.
- [ ] Rolling workforce plan covers age profile, skills mix, and attrition risk for critical professions, refreshed at least every two to three years.
- [ ] Succession and knowledge-transfer arrangements are funded and scheduled for roles facing near-term retirement risk.
- [ ] Volunteer coordination, where relied upon, is a funded, named responsibility, not an informal addition to paid staff's workload.
- [ ] Collective bargaining approach is set out in a multi-year framework where fiscally possible, rather than renegotiated from scratch each year.
- [ ] Pay equity is audited at role level, not only through organisation-wide averages.
- [ ] Frontline managers hold real, resourced authority over recruitment and retention decisions within the workforce plan.
- [ ] Staff have been told plainly what the organisation can and cannot promise on pay, security, and progression.
